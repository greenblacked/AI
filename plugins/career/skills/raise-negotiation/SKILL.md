# Raise Negotiation

Done looks like a written request with one figure and an effective date, in front of a manager who knows what they need to take it upward, and a written answer: yes, or the conditions under which it becomes yes and a date to revisit.

The usual failure is timing. By the time review season starts, the budget pool may already be sized, so the manager has nothing to give and the request is read as a complaint. The second failure is a case that mixes "I work hard" with "the market pays more", which gives the manager no clean argument to repeat to the person who approves it. This skill separates the two and starts early.

## Hard gates

- **Do not invent figures.** Market data points, the internal band and any outside offer are the user's facts. Where one is missing, write `[TK: number]` and ask the specific question that fills it.
- **Use an outside offer as leverage only if the user would take it.** A bluff is checkable and a bluff that is called ends the conversation badly.
- **Procedure, not legal or tax advice.** Pay-transparency rules and notice terms vary by country and region; where it matters, say so and have the user check locally.

## Scope

Use for: pay rises, promotions, level changes, a promotion that arrived without matching pay, and a good review followed by a small increase.

Do not use for: a new employer's offer, setting bands as a manager, writing reviews, or building the day-to-day relationship with a manager.

## Workflow

### 1. Fix timing

Ask when the compensation cycle runs, when budgets are set and when the manager submits recommendations. Open the conversation a few months before the manager submits compensation recommendations, so the manager can plan the ask into this cycle's budget, not search for room in a locked one. Where the cycle is unknown, ask the manager directly; where the answer is "any time", pick a point after a visible delivery rather than during a crunch. If the budget is already locked, say so and aim the request at the next cycle, with an interim step (scope, a title or a mid-cycle review date) agreed now.

### 2. Build the performance and scope case

One document, dated evidence from the last 12 months, built around one claim: the user already does work that belongs to the next level. For each item give what was owned, the outcome, who can confirm it, and the date. Include scope the user took on beyond the role description. Compare against the written level criteria if the company has them; where it has none, ask the manager what separates the current level from the next. Cut effort that is not outcome. Read `references/case-templates.md` for the evidence table.

Do not put market figures in this document. A performance case that cites the market reads as pressure.

### 3. Build the market case separately

A second document with three or more current external data points for the same level and location, each with a source and a date (published salary bands, a recruiter's range, a salary survey, offers seen by peers where they are willing to share). If the internal band for the user's level is known, show where the user sits in it. Be honest about the spread: a range with a median is more credible than a favourable outlier. If the data is thin, say so and either gather more or lean on the performance case.

Keep the two documents apart. The manager may carry one without the other depending on what the approver asks.

### 4. Ask about process before asking for a number

In a 1:1, ask: what is the process, who decides, what constraints exist (budget pool, band maximum, promotion windows), and what evidence they would want. This makes the eventual request one the manager can carry upward, and it surfaces constraints that change the ask (a band maximum, for instance, makes a promotion the only route above it). Take notes and send them back as a summary in writing.

### 5. State one figure and an effective date, in writing

After the process conversation, send a short written request: the figure or band, the effective date, and a pointer to the two documents. Close with "what do you need from me to get this approved?" A range invites the lowest number in it, so give one figure unless the process requires a band; when a band is required, make the bottom a number the user would accept. See `references/case-templates.md` for the message.

### 6. Handle the answer

- **Yes:** get it in writing with the new figure, title and effective date; confirm when payroll will reflect it.
- **Yes, but smaller:** ask what closes the gap and whether the remainder can be a dated second step.
- **Not now:** ask for the conditions under which it becomes yes (specific outcomes, a level, a budget event) and a date to revisit, and ask for both in writing. A no without conditions is a no with no path.
- **No:** ask whether the constraint is budget, level or performance. Each has a different next move.

### 7. Decide the walk-away line beforehand

Before the conversation, the user writes down what they will do if the answer is no: stay and revisit, take a non-pay item (title, scope, review date), or begin looking. Decide this while calm. An outside offer is leverage only when the user would take it; if so, share the fact plainly and ask what the company can do, without a deadline made up for effect.

## What to hand back

The two documents in draft, the process questions for the 1:1, the written request, and the walk-away line. State what evidence is missing.

## Anti-patterns

**Asking after the numbers are locked.** The manager has no room and the answer is a polite no that sounds like a verdict.

**One document that argues effort and market together.** It gives the manager nothing they can quote and weakens both halves.

**Naming a number before understanding the process.** The figure may be above the band maximum or below what a promotion would unlock.

**Citing "new hires earn more" as the whole case.** It is often true and often emotionally right, but without level, location and sources it is an anecdote.

**An ultimatum with no intention behind it.** If called, the user has to leave or lose credibility.
