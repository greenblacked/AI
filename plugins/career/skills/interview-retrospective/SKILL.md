# Interview Retrospective

A good retrospective ends with a short, specific change to practice, based on evidence from the loop and from earlier loops, rather than a vague resolution to try harder.

The usual failures are two. The first is judging the loop by its outcome: a rejection feels like proof of a skill gap and an offer feels like proof of none, though loops are noisy and interviewers disagree. The second is fixing the most recent result. One bad round tells little; a pattern across three loops tells a lot. Memory also decays within days and rewrites itself to be kinder or harsher, so the record has to be made while it is fresh.

## Scope

Use for: writing up a finished loop, interpreting feedback, choosing what to practise, tracking results across loops, and deciding whether to keep the target or change it.

Do not use for: preparing for a coding round (technical-interview-prep), preparing applications or explaining a gap (job-search), take-home work (take-home-assignment), negotiating (offer-negotiation).

## Workflow

### 1. Record within 24 hours

While memory is fresh, write down every question asked and the answer as you remember giving it, in order, per round. Include what the interviewer did: hints given, follow-ups, silences, interruptions. Mark the moments where you felt the round turn. Do not assess yet; the point is a raw record, because assessments written first colour what you later recall. Use the template in `references/loop-log.md`.

### 2. Classify each miss

Take each question where you are unsure it went well and assign a single cause:

| Cause | Sign |
| --- | --- |
| Knowledge gap | You did not know the concept or technique |
| Structure | You knew it but the answer wandered, skipped clarifying questions or lacked an order |
| Communication | The reasoning was correct but unclear, silent or hard to follow |
| Time and nerves | You ran out of time, froze or rushed |

Be strict: if a question could fit two, ask which fix would have changed the result. A knowledge gap needs study; the other three need rehearsal, and mixing them up sends effort to the wrong place.

### 3. Ask the recruiter one specific question

"Any feedback?" yields a template reply. Instead ask a single question the recruiter can answer without a committee: "Was the main concern the depth of my system design answer, or something in how I communicated it?" or "Which round was the weakest signal?" Send it within a few days, politely, once. Treat any reply as one data point and compare it with your own record; companies often give little or nothing for legal reasons, and silence is not information about you. Read `references/loop-log.md` for wording.

### 4. Choose at most three drills

Find the dominant cause across all rounds (the most frequent; on a tie, the one that cost the latest round) and pick no more than three drills tied to it, each with a measure and a date. Examples: for structure, answer ten questions aloud with a fixed opening framework and review a recording; for a knowledge gap, a bounded study plan on the one topic missed; for nerves, two mock interviews with a stranger before the next loop. More than three drills means none gets done. Anything outside the dominant cause waits.

### 5. Keep a table across loops

Maintain a table with one row per round: company, date, round type, outcome if known, dominant cause. Compute pass rate by round type as passes over known outcomes (leave unknown outcomes out of both counts; companies often report only the overall result), for example coding, system design, behavioural, hiring-manager. Where a round type has fewer than about three known outcomes, use the dominant self-classified cause instead. After three loops, the pattern sets the plan, not the latest result. If coding rounds pass and behavioural rounds fail across three companies, the plan is behavioural, whatever the last rejection said.

### 6. Set a stop point

Decide in advance, say after another three loops, what result means keep revising and what means change the approach. If a round type keeps failing despite targeted drills measured over that span, consider whether the target role is the right one: a different level, domain or company size. Changing target is not failure; continuing unchanged after a clear pattern is. Write the decision date down.

## Hand back

```text
Loop record (per round: questions, answers, interviewer behaviour)
Classification of each miss with the cause
The one question to send the recruiter
At most three drills, each with a measure and date
Updated table of pass rate by round type
Stop point and decision date
```

## Anti-patterns

**Waiting a week to write it up.** The record softens or hardens, and the detail that mattered is gone.

**Taking vague feedback as a diagnosis.** "Not enough signal" says nothing about which skill. Compare it with your own record before changing anything.

**Drilling everything.** A list of eight drills is a plan to feel busy. Tie effort to the dominant cause.

**Changing course after one loop.** One result is noise; wait for the pattern across three.

**Treating a rejection as a verdict on ability.** Loops vary in interviewers, bars and headcount. The retrospective is for finding what you can change.
