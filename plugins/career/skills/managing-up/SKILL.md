# Managing Up

Done looks like a manager who can describe the user's work accurately to their own boss, who hears bad news from the user first, and who makes decisions quickly because the user brings them in a form that can be decided.

The usual failure is waiting. People assume a manager knows what they do, what they are blocked on and how they like to be told things, then are surprised when the manager guesses wrong. A manager is reading several people at once with partial information; the user's job is to make their own signal cheap to read. Nothing here is about flattery or visibility for its own sake. Each step removes a specific kind of surprise.

## Scope

Use for: setting up or repairing the standing relationship with a manager or a senior stakeholder who holds decisions, bringing bad news upward, disagreeing and committing, getting priorities to stop shifting, and asking for more scope.

Do not use for: scripting one particular hard conversation, writing a review or promotion packet for a report (`growth-review`), formal status reports to a group, or asking for pay, level or a promotion case for yourself (`raise-negotiation`).

## Workflow

### 1. Diagnose which problem it is

Ask what is actually happening, because the fixes differ. Sort the situation into one of four:

| Symptom | Likely cause | Go to |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Manager is vague about what the user does or achieves | No visible signal | Steps 2 and 5 |
| Priorities keep changing | No agreed definition of success, or manager is passing down pressure unfiltered | Steps 2 and 6 |
| Bad news lands late or badly | No agreed channel, or the user waited | Step 3 |
| Disagreements go nowhere or sour | Raised in public, or re-raised without a new fact | Step 4 |

If the manager is hostile or the problem is conduct rather than communication, say so: this skill improves a workable relationship and does not fix a bad one. Point at the person's HR route or a skip-level conversation instead of coaching around it.

### 2. Write the working agreement

Propose a one-page document, drafted by the user and discussed in a 1:1, built from five questions asked of the manager. Draft the user's own answer to each first, so the manager is reacting to something rather than inventing from blank.

1. What does success in this role look like in six months?
2. What do you most want to hear early?
3. How do you prefer to be told bad news (channel, format, time of day)?
4. Which decisions are mine and which are yours?
5. What do you want me to stop doing?

Question four removes most ambiguity: write down the decisions the user will take and report afterwards, the ones to be consulted on first, and the ones that are the manager's. Question five is the one people skip and it is the most revealing. Keep the page to one side; a longer one is not read. Read `references/working-agreement.md` for the template and a worked example.

Send it before the meeting as a draft ("I wrote down how I think we work best; please mark it up"), and file the agreed version where both can find it.

### 3. Bring bad news early and in a fixed shape

Within a day of knowing, tell the manager. A day is the point at which the manager can still change the outcome and the news is still the user's to frame; after a week the same news reads as concealment. Use this order, because the manager's first questions are always the next item on the list:

1. What happened (one or two sentences, facts only).
2. Impact so far (customers, dates, money, people).
3. What I have done already.
4. The options.
5. My recommendation.
6. What I need from you.

Say the recommendation even when unsure; a manager given only options has to become the owner of the problem. If a number is unknown, say what is unknown and when it will be known. Do not open with an apology or a build-up, and do not bury the impact in the history.

### 4. Disagree privately, then commit

Raise the disagreement in a 1:1 before any group meeting. Name the specific decision, give the evidence and the cost of being wrong, and state what you would do instead. Ask what the manager knows that you do not; sometimes the answer ends the disagreement and sometimes it sharpens it.

Once the decision is made, commit visibly: support it in front of the team and do not relitigate in side channels. A person who commits fully on the third decision gets heard on the fourth. Escalate a second time only on a new fact, and say that is what it is ("this was not known on Tuesday"). Escalating on the same facts converts a disagreement into a character reference.

### 5. Send a weekly three-line update, unasked

Once a week, in the same place, three lines: done, next, blocked. Keep each line to a sentence that the manager could repeat to their own boss. The purpose is accuracy of the representation of the user's work upward, so write the lines in outcomes ("migration of billing reads finished, error rate unchanged") rather than activity ("worked on migration"). Blocked is the line that earns its keep: name the person or decision that unblocks it. If nothing is blocked, write "nothing", which is itself information.

Stop the update only if the manager asks; a manager who never replies is still reading.

### 6. Stabilise shifting priorities

When priorities change, do not absorb the change silently. Put the current ordered list in the weekly update, and when a new request arrives, answer with the trade: "I can do this; it displaces X, which was due Friday. Do you want that?" This makes the cost of the change visible to the person who ordered it and gives the manager something to push back with upward. Ask once, in the 1:1, which of the manager's priorities are fixed and which are drafts.

### 7. Ask for more scope

Request scope as a proposal with a bounded first step, not as a wish. Name the area, what you will own, what you will stop or hand off to make room, and how the manager will know it is going well. Offer a first piece that finishes within a quarter. Scope given on a vague wish tends to arrive as extra work with no authority.

### 8. Review the agreement each quarter

Once a quarter, ask for feedback on the working agreement itself: is the update useful, is anything missing, has the answer to any of the five questions changed. Revise the page and date it. Relationships drift when the agreement is treated as finished.

## What to hand back

The draft working agreement, the bad-news message if one is pending, the weekly update template filled for this week, and a short list of what the user should say in the next 1:1. State plainly which facts came from the user and which are assumed.

## Anti-patterns

**Surprising the manager in a group.** A disagreement or a missed date first heard in front of peers forces the manager to defend or attack. Private first, always.

**Bad news as a status update.** Burying a slipped date in a long update means the manager discovers it later and trusts every future update less.

**Relitigating a decided matter.** Raising the same point in new channels is read as refusal to commit, and it uses the credit needed for the next real disagreement.

**An update that lists activity.** A list of tasks lets the manager guess at value. Outcomes let them quote it.

**Asking for more scope without giving something up.** It reads as ambition without judgement, and the manager has no answer to what happens to the existing work.
