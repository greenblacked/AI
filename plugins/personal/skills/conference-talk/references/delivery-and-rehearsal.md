# Delivery and Rehearsal

Read this in the two weeks before the talk. Everything here is mechanical and learnable;
none of it depends on being a naturally confident speaker.

## The rehearsal protocol

Three full runs is the floor. Five is where most talks stop improving.

| Run | Purpose | What to do with the result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Discover that the talk does not exist yet | Expect it to be a mess and to run long or short by a wide margin. Fix structure, not wording |
| 2 | Fix the transitions | Note every place you said "and, um, so anyway". That is a missing transition line; write it |
| 3 | Time it properly | Record the split at each movement boundary. These are your mid-talk checkpoints |
| 4 | In front of a person | Ask for the takeaway in their words afterwards. If it is not your sentence, the structure is wrong |
| 5 | Polish | Opening and closing verbatim-comfortable, demo fallback exercised, timing confirmed |

Rules that make the runs worth doing:

- Out loud, at speaking volume. Silent rehearsal runs at roughly twice the pace and hides
  every sentence you cannot physically say.
- Standing, advancing the real slides, with the clicker if you have one.
- Do not stop to fix things mid-run. Note it and carry on; stopping means you never
  practise recovering, which is the skill you will actually need.
- Record one run and watch it once. It is unpleasant and it is the fastest way to find the
  pacing problem, the filler word and the habit of talking to the screen.
- Rehearse the opening until it is automatic. The first ninety seconds are where nerves
  peak, and a memorised opening carries you through them.
- Rehearse the demo, including the failure: practise saying "that is not working, here is
  the recording" and moving on without apology.

## Cutting to time

Target well under the slot. A 30 minute slot means aiming at 24; a 45 minute slot means
aiming at 35. The gap absorbs the late start, the microphone, the extra sentence per
section, and the two questions asked during the talk.

Decide the cuts in advance and write them down, in order. Deciding on stage costs you the
ending.

| Cut first | Cut last |
| --- | --- |
| The second example that makes the same point | The opening |
| Background the audience mostly has | The takeaway, stated at each boundary |
| The detour you find interesting | The ending |
| The demo, if the recording says it anyway | The one number the argument rests on |

If you are over by more than about 20 percent, that is a structural problem rather than a
trimming problem: one movement has to go entirely.

## The week before

- Slides finished, so the last week is rehearsal rather than design.
- Fallback recording made and tested at full screen.
- Deck exported to PDF as a backup, and on a USB drive as a backup to the backup.
- Fonts embedded or the deck opened on a machine that is not yours.
- Speaker notes reduced to structure and numbers. A script gets read; bullets get spoken.
- Travel and sleep planned so you are not delivering on four hours.

## The room and the equipment

Arrive early enough to test rather than to hope.

- The connector. Bring your own adapters for the two most likely ports and do not rely on
  the room having them.
- The resolution. Check that your terminal and diagrams are legible from the back row,
  from the back row, not from the stage.
- The microphone: handheld, lapel or headset changes what you can do with your hands.
  Wear something a lapel microphone can clip to.
- Where the confidence monitor is, and whether it shows notes or mirrors the screen.
- Where the countdown clock is, if there is one.
- Whether the session is recorded and whether the recording captures the screen.
- Water within reach, phone silenced, notifications off on the laptop, screensaver
  disabled.

## Voice, body and nerves

- **Pace.** Deliberately slower than feels right. Nerves accelerate you by an amount you
  cannot perceive from the inside. This is the single highest-value adjustment available
  mid-talk.
- **Pauses.** Two seconds after an important sentence. It feels like an eternity on stage
  and reads as authority from the room. It is also when the sentence lands.
- **Volume and end of sentences.** Nerves make people trail off. Finish the sentence at
  the same volume you started it.
- **Hands.** Give them a job: the clicker, a gesture at the diagram. Pockets and lectern
  gripping both read as tension.
- **Feet.** Plant them. Wandering and rocking are the two habits recordings expose most.
- **Eyes.** Three points in the room, rotating. In a dark room aim where the faces are.
- **Filler.** A pause instead of "um". You do not owe the room continuous sound.
- **Nerves.** They are not visible at anything like the intensity they are felt. Racing
  heart, dry mouth, shaking hands: none of it reaches row four. Believing that is what
  stops the second-order panic about looking nervous.
- If your hands shake, do not hold a laser pointer or a sheet of paper; both amplify it.

## Q and A

Repeat every question before answering, for the room and for the recording. Answer in
about a minute, two at most.

| Situation | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| You do not know | Say so. "I do not know — find me afterwards and we can work it out." It is the most credible sentence available to you |
| A statement rather than a question | Let them finish, extract anything answerable, answer that in one sentence, take the next hand |
| Hostile tone | Answer the technical content, ignore the tone, do not get drawn. The room is on your side |
| Very specific to their system | One sentence of principle, then offer to talk afterwards |
| A question you covered | Answer it again briefly rather than pointing at the slide. Someone missed it, and others did too |
| Nobody asks anything | Have one ready: "the thing people usually ask me is..." |
| Someone monopolising | "Let me take one more and then I will be at the back if you want to keep going" |

Keep the takeaway slide up throughout. It is on screen for the whole of Q and A, which is
several minutes of free reinforcement.

## After the talk

- Post the slides within a couple of days, with speaker notes attached so the deck makes
  sense to people who were not there.
- Follow up on every question you could not answer. Those people are the most engaged
  audience the talk produced.
- Write down, the same day, what ran long, which slide confused people, and which question
  came up more than once. The repeated question belongs in the next version of the talk.
- Watch the recording once, with a notebook, then leave it alone.
