# Scripts for the offer conversation

Skeletons, not lines to memorise. Rewrite each in the user's own vocabulary before they
say it — a sentence someone would not say aloud collapses the moment the conversation
moves off-script, and the recruiter can hear the difference. Bracketed slots must be
filled with the user's real facts; leave `[TK: ...]` where a fact is missing rather than
inventing one.

## Asked for a number early

Redirect once, warmly:

> Happy to talk numbers. So we are anchored on the same thing — what is the band for
> this level? I would rather work from your range than guess at it.

Pressed a second time, give a range with its reason, floor set at a number the user would
sign:

> Based on posted ranges for [level] in [city] and the scope I have been carrying at
> [current employer] — [one line of scope evidence] — I have been working to
> [floor] to [ceiling], with the level and the rest of the package mattering as much
> as base.

Asked what they are currently on, where the user would rather not say:

> I would rather not anchor on my current salary — it reflects a role I took
> [three years ago / before the team doubled]. What I am looking for from a move is
> [range], for the reasons above.

Some jurisdictions prohibit asking about salary history and some require a range on
request; this varies and changes, so check locally rather than asserting it in the
conversation. Declining to answer is available everywhere and is not rude.

## Asked for salary history

If the user chooses to answer, answer with total compensation rather than base, and
include what would be forfeited by leaving:

> My package is [base] plus [bonus at actual payout] and [equity], and I would be walking
> away from [unvested amount] vesting in [month]. That is the number a move has to beat.

## The consolidated ask

By email, so it can be forwarded to whoever approves it. Enthusiasm, ask, reason, close.

> Thanks for sending this through — I am genuinely excited about [specific thing:
> the platform rebuild, the team, the problem]. I want to say yes, and there are
> [two/three] things I would like to close first, in priority order:
>
> 1. **Level.** [Evidence: scope owned, decisions made, an artefact at the target level.]
>    I think this is a [target level] role and I would like it reviewed.
> 2. **Base.** [Number.] [Reason: band data, competing offer, current package.]
> 3. **Sign-on.** [Number], to cover [unvested bonus vesting in March / notice period /
>    relocation].
>
> If you can get to [the shape that closes it], I am ready to sign and we can talk start
> dates. Happy to jump on a call if that is easier.

Then stop. Do not send a follow-up softening it the next morning.

By phone, the same shape in three sentences, followed by silence:

> This is the team I want to join. Where I have landed is that [ask], because [reason].
> Can you make that work?

## Asking about level

Ask before the offer where possible, and again at offer stage if the level surprises:

> What separates [level] from [level+1] here, and what would you need to see from me to
> place me at the higher one?

If a re-levelling loop is offered, weigh it honestly: it can raise the offer, it can
confirm the lower level, and in some companies it can lower an offer already made. Ask
directly whether the current offer is protected during a re-level.

## Asking for more time

> This is great news and I want to give you a proper answer rather than a fast one. Can I
> come back to you by [day]? I have a final round with one other company on [day], and I
> would rather decide with everything in front of me.

Naming the other process is honest and normal when it is true. Where there is no other
process, "I want to talk it through with my partner and read the documents properly" is
both true and sufficient.

## An exploding deadline

> I understand there is a process. Realistically I cannot make a decision of this size in
> [24 hours] — I would need until [day]. If the offer cannot hold until then, tell me now
> and I will give you an answer with the information I have.

The last sentence matters: it hands the deadline back rather than fighting it. A firm
refusal to extend a reasonable request is data about the company, and it is worth saying
so to the user plainly — companies that will not give a week to consider are usually not
the ones that will be flexible later.

## Accepting

> Delighted — I accept. Could you send the written offer with [the agreed base, level,
> sign-on and start date] included, plus the equity documents and the full benefits
> summary? I will sign as soon as I have read them, and I will hand in my notice
> afterwards.

The last clause is not a formality: it tells the recruiter the sequence and removes any
pressure to resign early.

## The counteroffer from the current employer

To the current manager, if the user wants to hear it out:

> I want to be straight with you: I have accepted [or: I have an offer]. The reason I
> started looking is [scope / the manager relationship / the work / money]. I am not
> looking to be outbid — if there were a version of this job that fixed [reason], I would
> want to talk about that specifically.

For the user: a counteroffer proves the budget was always there and it was not offered
until leaving was on the table. Ask what changes beyond the number, whether it is written
down with a date, and what happens to it at the next planning round. Where the only
reason for leaving was pay, and the counteroffer meets it, accepting can be a reasonable
decision — but say plainly that the user will be a known flight risk, and that the offer
they walk away from will not be available again.

## Declining

Quickly, by phone or short note, with a real reason and no padding:

> Thank you — I have decided to take another offer. It came down to [one honest reason:
> the scope of the role, the domain, the compensation]. I enjoyed the conversations with
> [name] and I hope our paths cross again; I would be glad to stay in touch.

Do not disappear on a process, do not invent a reason, and do not use one offer's decline
to negotiate the other after the fact. The market is small, the recruiter will move
companies twice in five years, and the hiring manager may run a loop the user wants in
three.
