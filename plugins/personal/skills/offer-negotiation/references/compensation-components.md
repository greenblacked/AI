# Compensation components, decomposed

None of this is tax, legal or financial advice. Equity taxation, retirement-account rules
and the enforceability of contract clauses vary by country and by state or province, and
they change. Where the amounts are material, the user should have a professional in their
own jurisdiction read the documents before signing.

## Guaranteed, probable, lottery

Sort every component into one of three buckets before comparing anything.

| Bucket | Contains | How to treat it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Guaranteed cash | Base, contractual sign-on, contractual allowances | Add it up; this is the number that pays rent |
| Probable cash | Bonus at historical payout, pension match, public-company RSUs | Discount it, state the discount, keep it in its own column |
| Lottery | Private-company options and RSUs, promised future refreshes, "we will revisit in six months" | Do not add to cash. Describe the distribution and the terms |

The failure this prevents: a single "total comp" figure that mixes a certain 95k with a
notional 60k of private shares and reads as 155k, against which a certain 120k looks poor.

## Base and band placement

Ask for the band for the level, and where in it the offer sits. Bottom-of-band is the
easiest thing for a recruiter to move and the most common place for an offer to land,
particularly for candidates who did not ask. Mid-band is a normal target for someone
hired at level; top-of-band usually needs either scarcity or a competing offer, and it
also means the next raise is a promotion or nothing.

## Bonus: target versus what paid

Target percentage is a plan. Ask what actually paid out company-wide for each of the last
two or three years, and whether it is funded on company performance, team performance,
individual rating, or all three multiplied together — a 15% target that has paid 15%,
9% and 0% is not a 15% bonus. Ask also what happens in the first year: many schemes
pro-rate, some require employment on the payout date, which can mean a full year worked
and nothing paid if the user leaves in the wrong month.

## Equity: the questions and what each answer means

Ask for these in writing. A recruiter who cannot answer them will get the answers; one
who refuses has told you something.

| Ask | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| Number of units or options, not a currency amount | A dollar figure hides the valuation assumption doing all the work |
| Type: RSU, ISO, NSO, growth shares, phantom | Determines when tax lands and whether money is needed up front |
| Strike price (options) | The gap between strike and value is the whole instrument |
| Latest preferred price and latest common/409A valuation | Preferred is what investors paid with protections attached; common is what the user holds. Quoting equity at the preferred price overstates it |
| Total shares outstanding, fully diluted | A grant is only interpretable as a fraction of the company |
| Vesting schedule and cliff | Four years with a one-year cliff is a common shape; check for back-loaded schedules where most vests in years three and four |
| Refresh policy, and what refreshes actually looked like last cycle | Without refreshes, total compensation falls off a cliff in year four |
| What happens on leaving | Unvested is nearly always forfeited; what happens to vested is the question |
| Acquisition and IPO terms | Single or double-trigger acceleration, treatment in an all-cash sale, and lock-up |
| Post-termination exercise window | See below |
| Liquidation preference stack | With enough preference ahead of common, a mid-priced sale pays common holders nothing |

## The exercise window

The term that most often converts a paper number into nothing. The traditional window is
90 days after leaving: the user must exercise within it or the options lapse. Exercising
means paying the strike price on every vested option in cash, and in many jurisdictions
paying tax on the spread in the same year, for shares that cannot be sold. Someone with
40,000 vested options at a $2 strike needs $80,000 plus a tax bill to keep them, or walks
away with nothing after four years.

Extended windows exist — some companies offer seven or ten years — and asking whether
this company has one is a legitimate, well-informed question. It is occasionally
negotiable, more often in early-stage companies. Where the window is 90 days, the honest
framing is that the equity is worth something only if the user stays until liquidity, or
can fund an exercise later.

## Sign-on, and its clawback

Sign-on is where recruiters most often have discretion, because it is one-time money that
does not disturb the band or set a precedent for the next review. Ask when it is paid
(first payslip, split across two years, on the anniversary), and ask for the clawback
terms in writing: repayment is common if the user leaves inside twelve or twenty-four
months, and it is sometimes gross rather than net, meaning the repayment exceeds what was
received after tax. A sign-on used to close a gap left by unvested bonus at the current
employer should at minimum cover the gross amount forfeited.

## Pension, retirement match and benefits that are real money

Convert to an annual figure and put it in the comparison:

- Employer pension or 401(k) match — percentage, cap, and vesting of the employer part.
  A 6% match on a 100k salary is 6k a year that never appears in a "total comp" headline.
- Health cover, and the marginal cost of adding a partner or children.
- Leave: days above statutory, whether it carries over, whether the company actually
  takes it, and any sabbatical entitlement.
- Parental leave beyond statutory, which for someone planning a family can dwarf a
  base-salary difference.
- Learning budget, conference travel, equipment allowance.
- Visa sponsorship, relocation, immigration legal costs, and — where the user's status is
  tied to the employer — what happens if the job ends. This one is not money, it is risk.

## Building the comparison

Produce three lines per offer, never one:

    Guaranteed:  base + contractual sign-on (+ pension match, marked)
    Probable:    bonus at historical payout, public RSUs at today's price
    Upside:      [units] of [type], [terms], valued as a range with a stated zero case

Then say which line the decision actually turns on. In most comparisons between a stable
company and an early-stage one it is the guaranteed line plus the non-compensation
columns from Workflow 1, and the equity is a tiebreaker the user is choosing to buy.
