# Booking mechanics

Read this when comparing fares, when a connection looks tight, when deciding how much
protection a booking actually carries, or when a leg has already gone wrong and you need to
know what someone owes you. Regulations and thresholds change; check the regulator's own
page before quoting a figure to someone who is about to rely on it.

## What a ticket is

A ticket is a contract of carriage between the passenger and the ticketing airline for the
whole journey on it. Everything that matters about disruption follows from that sentence:

- The carrier is obliged to get you to the final destination on the ticket, not to the
  next airport on it. A missed connection caused by its own late first leg is its problem
  to solve, at no charge.
- Duty of care — meals, refreshment, and a hotel with transfers where an overnight is
  forced — attaches to the ticket, and in several jurisdictions is owed regardless of
  whether the cause was within the airline's control.
- A second ticket is a second contract with someone who has no obligation arising from
  what happened on the first.

The price difference between one ticket and two is therefore the price of that obligation.
Sometimes it is worth paying for and sometimes it is not, but it should be a decision.

## Ticket structures and what each owes you

| Structure | How to recognise it | Protection when the first leg is late | Where it fails |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Single ticket, single carrier | One booking reference, one airline, all legs on one confirmation | Rebooked free to the final destination; duty of care applies | Only where the carrier has few onward options. |
| Single ticket, interline or codeshare partners | One booking reference, legs operated by different airlines under one ticket number | Identical: the ticket is one contract regardless of operator | Baggage rules can differ per operating carrier; check the lowest allowance. |
| Two tickets, same airline | Two booking references with the same airline | No contractual obligation, but the airline usually helps because the cost of refusing is a public argument | Discretionary, and discretion disappears on a full flight. |
| Two tickets, different airlines (self-transfer) | Two booking references, bags usually not through-checked | Nothing. The second fare is lost and a replacement is bought at the walk-up price | Any delay at all, plus any immigration or baggage step in between. |
| Self-transfer with a platform guarantee | Sold as "protected" or "guaranteed" by a booking platform | Whatever the platform's terms say, which is typically a rebooking on the next available flight, not compensation | Terms often exclude weather, require you to notify within a window, and cap the hotel. |

For a self-transfer, size the buffer to the replacement, not to the walk. Ask: if this
first leg lands three hours late, when is the next flight I could take, and what does it
cost? If the answer is "tomorrow", the buffer is an overnight and a hotel, and the saving
has probably already gone.

## Minimum connection time

The published minimum connection time is the shortest interval at which an airport's
systems will sell a connection. It assumes an on-time arrival, no gate change, no queue,
and a passenger who moves briskly and knows the way. It is a legal floor, not a safe one.

Typical published values, which vary by airport and terminal and should be checked for the
specific airport:

| Connection type | Commonly published | Plan for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Domestic to domestic, same terminal | 45–60 minutes | 75–90 minutes |
| International to international, same terminal, no immigration | 60–90 minutes | 2 hours |
| Any connection changing terminal | 90–120 minutes | 2.5 hours |
| Any connection clearing immigration and rechecking bags | 90–120 minutes | 3 hours |
| Two separate tickets | Not applicable; no minimum exists | Overnight, or the next-flight interval |

Additional facts that move the number: the last flight of the day has no recovery option,
so it should carry more buffer than a midday connection; airports with a single daily bank
of connections concentrate the risk; some countries require clearing immigration on
arrival even for onward international flights, which no timetable shows.

## Reading a fare rule

The cheap fare and the flexible fare differ in four places, and the difference is usually
written in terms nobody defines on the booking page:

- **Changeable.** Whether the date can move at all, the change fee, and — the part people
  miss — that the fare difference is payable on top. A "free change" on a fare that has
  since doubled costs the doubling.
- **Refundable.** Whether cancelling returns money, returns a credit, or returns only the
  taxes. Taxes-only is the common outcome on the cheapest fares and is a small fraction of
  what was paid.
- **Credit terms.** How long a credit is valid, whether it is transferable to another
  passenger, and whether it is restricted to the same route or fare class. An
  eighteen-month non-transferable credit is worth far less than its face value.
- **No-show rule.** Missing one leg frequently cancels every subsequent leg on the same
  ticket automatically. This is what makes skipping the first leg of a return a way to
  lose the return.

Record all four at the moment of booking, while the page is open. Retrieving them later
means finding the fare basis code and reading the rule text, which is slower than it
sounds.

## When a flight is delayed or cancelled

Two things are distinct and are routinely confused: the **refund or rebooking**, which is
about the fare, and the **compensation**, which is a statutory penalty on the airline.

**Refund and rebooking.** In broad terms, a cancelled flight entitles you to a choice
between a rebooking and a refund of the unflown portion. United States rules require
automatic cash refunds for cancelled and significantly changed flights, with the
significance threshold set in hours of schedule change, and the refund must be in the
original form of payment rather than a voucher. European rules give a similar choice plus
duty of care. Check the current thresholds with the relevant regulator: the US Department
of Transportation, or the national enforcement body for the European regime.

**Compensation.** The European regime (and the closely modelled United Kingdom one) pays a
fixed sum banded by flight distance for long delays at arrival and for cancellations
notified inside a notice period, applying to departures from those territories on any
airline and to arrivals on their carriers. It does not apply where the cause is an
extraordinary circumstance outside the airline's control, which covers most weather and
air-traffic control events but not, under settled case law, ordinary technical faults. The
United States has no equivalent compensation scheme for delay; its protections are refunds
and, since recent rulemaking, fee transparency.

**Duty of care** is separate again and is owed during the wait: meals proportionate to the
delay, and accommodation plus transfers where an overnight is forced. It does not stop
applying because the cause was extraordinary.

What to do at the time, in order: get rebooked first, because seats are finite and
compensation is not; keep the boarding passes, the delay notification and any receipt;
claim the statutory compensation directly with the airline before paying a claims company
a share of it.

## Accommodation and rail

- **Free cancellation windows differ per rate on the same property.** A non-refundable rate
  is typically 10–20% cheaper and is worth it only when the dates genuinely cannot move.
- **Put every cancellation deadline in the calendar** with the booking reference in the
  title, an alert two days before, and the cancellation link in the notes.
- **Pay-at-property versus prepaid** changes what a dispute looks like. A prepaid booking
  through an intermediary means two parties can each point at the other.
- **Rail differs sharply by country.** Advance fares are usually train-specific and
  non-refundable; flexible fares are valid on any train that day. In some networks a
  reservation is compulsory even with a pass, and reservation quotas for pass holders sell
  out long before the train does.
- **Rail-to-flight connections carry no protection at all** unless sold as a single
  through ticket. Treat them as a self-transfer and buffer accordingly.

## Insurance types

| Type | What it pays for | The number that matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Emergency medical and evacuation | Treatment abroad and transport to adequate care or home | Evacuation is the catastrophic cost — commonly quoted in the tens of thousands and reaching six figures for a long-haul air ambulance. Check the per-person limit, not the headline. |
| Trip cancellation | The non-refundable costs when a covered reason stops you going | It pays only for listed reasons — illness, injury, death, jury service, specific disruptions. Changing your mind is not one. |
| Trip interruption | Getting home early and the unused portion | Check whether it pays the original fare class or an economy replacement. |
| Cancel for any reason | Part of the cost, whatever the reason | Typically 50–75% reimbursed, must usually be bought within 14–21 days of the first deposit, and costs a significant premium. |
| Baggage and delay | Replacement of lost items and essentials during a delay | Per-item caps are low; valuables and electronics are usually sub-limited or excluded. |
| Annual multi-trip | The same cover across a year | Watch the maximum trip length per journey, commonly 21–31 days. |

## What insurance does not cover

The three exclusions that deny real claims:

- **Pre-existing medical conditions**, unless declared and accepted. The definition
  typically reaches back over a lookback period of months and includes conditions under
  investigation, awaiting results, or with a recent change in medication — not only those
  formally diagnosed. Non-disclosure voids the claim and often the whole policy.
- **Travel against official advice.** A foreign-ministry advisory against travel to a
  region, or against all but essential travel, generally voids cover for that region. The
  advisory can change after you book, which is itself a reason to check it before
  departure.
- **Undeclared activities.** Motorcycling and scooter hire (often also requiring a valid
  licence for that class and a helmet), diving below a depth, off-piste skiing, climbing,
  and organised contact sport. Scooter accidents abroad are a classic uninsured
  catastrophic claim.

Further common exclusions worth naming: incidents where alcohol or drugs were a factor;
losses not reported to police within a stated window, usually 24 hours; unattended
possessions; cash above a low limit; anything the trip provider has already refunded; and
pregnancy beyond a stated week.

## Cover you may already have

Check before buying, and check the limits rather than the existence:

- **Credit card travel cover**, which usually requires the trip to have been paid for on
  that card, sometimes in full. Limits are typically well below a private evacuation.
- **Reciprocal state healthcare arrangements** between specific countries, which cover
  treatment on the same terms as a resident. They do not cover repatriation, and they do
  not cover private facilities — which is where an ambulance may take you.
- **Home contents insurance**, which sometimes extends to possessions away from home.
- **An employer's business travel policy**, which occasionally extends to leisure days
  attached to a work trip.

## Making a claim

- Report to the police within the required window for theft; the reference number is
  usually a condition of payment.
- Keep receipts for everything bought as a result of the disruption, including the ones
  that feel too small to matter.
- Notify the insurer before incurring a large medical cost where the policy requires
  pre-authorisation; a facility chosen without it can be excluded.
- Claim against the airline or provider first where a statutory right exists; insurers
  will deduct anything you could have recovered elsewhere.
- Submit inside the claim window, which is often 28 days from the incident and is enforced.
