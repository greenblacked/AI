# Entry requirements

Read this before booking anything non-refundable, and again if any traveller, passport,
route or date changes. Everything here is a procedure for finding the current answer, not
the answer itself: entry rules change with weeks of notice and any figure written down
here is a starting point to verify, not a fact to quote.

## The rule about sources

The answer to "do I need a visa" is enforced twice: at the check-in desk, by an airline
that pays a fine and the cost of your return flight if it carries an inadmissible
passenger, and at the border, by an officer with discretion. The airline's rule is the
one that decides whether you travel at all, and airlines apply rules conservatively.

Rank sources in this order, and never substitute a lower one for a higher one:

1. The destination government's immigration service or foreign ministry, in its own words.
2. The destination's embassy or consulate for the traveller's country of nationality.
3. The traveller's own foreign ministry travel-advice page for that destination.
4. The operating airline's travel-requirements tool.
5. Anything else, including aggregator sites, forums, and a previous trip.

Sources 1 to 4 are worth checking together, because disagreement between them is itself
information — usually that a rule changed recently and one of them has not caught up. When
they disagree, the conservative reading wins and the embassy is the tiebreak worth an email.

Record, for every check: the traveller's nationality, the destination, the URL, the date
checked, and the sentence that answered the question. A check with no date on it is
worthless in three months, which is roughly the planning horizon of a trip.

## The checking procedure

For each traveller, for each country entered or transited:

1. Determine the nationality on the passport that will actually be presented. Not the
   country of residence, not the second citizenship left at home.
2. Find the visa policy for that nationality for the intended purpose (tourism, business,
   visiting family, study) and duration.
3. Note which of the four shapes applies: visa-free, visa on arrival, electronic
   authorisation before travel, or a visa applied for in advance at a mission.
4. Note the permitted stay in days, and how it is counted. A "90 days in any 180" rule is
   a rolling window over previous visits, not a fresh allowance per entry.
5. Note the fee, the payment method accepted, and whether it is refundable on refusal.
   Visa-on-arrival counters frequently take cash only, in a specific currency.
6. Note the processing time, and treat the upper end as the real one.
7. Check the same for every transit point separately.
8. Re-check the whole set roughly two weeks before departure. Rules change; a check made
   at booking is not a check made at travel.

## Passport validity

Three independent rules, each of which can fail a passport whose expiry date looks fine.

**Six months beyond entry.** A large number of countries require the passport to remain
valid for at least six months from the date of entry. It is common across Asia, the Middle
East, Africa and parts of Latin America — Thailand, Vietnam, Indonesia and Malaysia all
state it in those terms. The United States applies a related but different test, six months
beyond the intended period of stay. Some countries use three months, some require validity
only for the duration of stay, and a few accept a passport valid on the day of exit. The
safe planning rule is that a passport with under six months remaining needs checking
country by country, and a passport with under three months remaining should be renewed
before the trip regardless.

**Counted from entry, and by a minority from departure.** The dominant rule bites on the
day you land, so a passport that clears it for a two-week trip clears it for a two-month
one. A few countries count the six months from the intended date of departure instead, and
on a long trip that difference is the whole of the margin. Read which one the destination
states; where its page does not say, plan on the later of the two dates.

**Issued within the last ten years.** Schengen area entry requires the passport to have
been issued less than ten years before the date of entry, and to be valid for at least
three months beyond the intended date of departure from the area. Passports renewed under
schemes that added unused months to the new document's expiry can satisfy the expiry test
and fail the issue-date test. Read the date of issue, not only the date of expiry.

**Damage.** A passport with water damage, a detached cover, a torn page or an unofficial
alteration can be refused as invalid at the discretion of the officer. If it looks damaged,
treat it as expired.

## Blank pages

Many countries require blank pages for the entry stamp and visa. One page is common; two
is required by a number of countries, South Africa among the strictest, and refusal of
entry for lack of pages happens. The pages must be the ones marked for visas — the
amendment, endorsement and observation pages at the back do not count, and neither does a
page with a stamp on one side only where the requirement is for a full blank leaf.

Count the genuinely blank visa pages in each passport during planning. Running out
mid-trip on a multi-country itinerary is a real failure mode; adding pages is not possible
in most countries that issue biometric passports, so the remedy is a new passport.

## Electronic travel authorisations

A growing number of destinations require an electronic authorisation before boarding even
where no visa is needed. They share a shape: applied for online days to weeks ahead, tied
to the passport, valid for a period or a number of entries, and carrying a fee. The
specific schemes, their fees and their start dates move — the United States, Canada,
Australia, New Zealand, the United Kingdom and the European Union all operate or are
introducing one — so find the current scheme and fee on the destination government's own
site rather than relying on any figure quoted second-hand.

Two cautions apply to all of them:

- **Apply through the official government site.** Every scheme attracts commercial
  look-alike sites that charge a markup for filling in the same form, and some collect
  passport data with no authority to do so. The official domain is on the government page
  you already found in step 1 of the procedure.
- **An authorisation is permission to board, not permission to enter.** It removes the
  visa requirement; it does not bind the officer at the border.

Note also that an authorisation is tied to the passport it was issued against. Renew the
passport and the authorisation does not follow.

## Transit

Transit is a separate permission from entry and is the most commonly missed requirement on
a trip.

- Some countries require a transit visa even when you never leave the international zone.
- Some have no international zone at all, so every passenger clears immigration and
  therefore needs an entry visa to change planes.
- Transit rules frequently depend on the nationality of the passenger, the destination
  country, the airline, and whether the baggage is checked through.
- Changing airport within a city is always an entry, never a transit.
- Two separate tickets usually force a baggage collection, which usually forces an entry.

Check the transit country's own immigration page for the traveller's nationality, and
check it for each transit point in both directions — return routings often differ.

## The facts that change the answer

Any of these changes the requirement, and each is worth asking about explicitly:

| Fact | Why it changes the answer |
| --- | --- |
| Dual nationality | The rule follows the passport presented; the other citizenship may make entry easier or may create an obligation on exit. |
| Residence permit in a third country | Many schemes waive visas for holders of certain residence permits, and the permit must then be carried. |
| A child travelling without both parents | Several countries require a notarised consent letter from the absent parent and a full birth certificate. |
| Previous refusal or overstay | Can invalidate eligibility for a visa-free or electronic scheme entirely. |
| Certain prior travel history | Some countries restrict eligibility for electronic authorisation based on previous visits to specific countries. |
| A recently issued passport | The authorisation, visa and any stored biometric data are tied to the old document. |
| Purpose of visit other than tourism | Paid work, study, journalism, research and volunteering usually need a different visa, even for a few days. |
| Criminal record | Declarable on several authorisation schemes and grounds for refusal. |

## Health requirements

Distinguish the two kinds, because only one can stop you boarding:

- **Mandatory**, enforced at the border: a yellow fever vaccination certificate is the
  most common, frequently triggered by having transited a country with transmission risk
  rather than by the destination itself. Polio certificates apply for some countries.
  Requirements appear on the destination government page and on the traveller's national
  travel-health service.
- **Recommended**, enforced by nothing but consequences: routine boosters, hepatitis,
  typhoid, rabies, malaria prophylaxis. These need a travel health appointment, and some
  courses take weeks to complete, so they are a lead time on the plan.

Carry prescription medication in its original labelled packaging with a copy of the
prescription, and check whether any of it is a controlled substance at the destination —
several common prescriptions, including some for attention disorders and strong
painkillers, are banned or require an import permit in specific countries.

## Proof the border may ask for

Officers can ask for evidence of the conditions of entry, and airlines sometimes check the
same items before boarding:

- **Onward or return ticket.** Commonly required for visa-free entry and routinely checked
  by airlines. A one-way ticket into a country with this rule is a boarding refusal.
- **Proof of accommodation** for the first nights, or an invitation letter with an
  address.
- **Sufficient funds**, sometimes stated as an amount per day, evidenced by a card
  statement or cash.
- **Travel insurance**, which is a formal entry condition in the Schengen area for visa
  applicants and in some other countries.

Have these available on the phone and on paper for the first entry. The paper copy matters
because this is exactly when the phone is out of battery.

## Lead times become dates

Every requirement with a processing time is a deadline on the plan, not a task. Convert
each into a date the moment it is discovered, and work backwards from the departure date
with the upper bound of the published processing time plus a week of slack:

| Requirement | What the date is |
| --- | --- |
| Passport renewal | Apply by departure minus the published service time, doubled, and remember the passport is unavailable while it is being processed — which blocks any visa application that needs it. |
| Visa applied for at a mission | Apply by departure minus the published processing time, plus travel time for the passport if it is posted. |
| Electronic authorisation | Apply at booking; approval is usually fast but can be referred for manual review. |
| Vaccination course | Start by departure minus the full course length plus the time to immunity. |
| Consent letter for a child | Notarisation needs an appointment and both parents. |

The sequencing trap is that a visa application requires surrendering the passport, so a
passport renewal and a visa application cannot run in parallel. Renew first, then apply.
