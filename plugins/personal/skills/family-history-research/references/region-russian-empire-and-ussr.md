# Region: the Russian Empire, the Soviet Union, Galicia and Bukovina

Read this at phase 3 when an event falls in these states or the ones that followed them.
Holding institutions are named by kind only. A statement without a marker was checked for this
skill; a statement marked "not independently verified for this skill" comes from general
knowledge and must be confirmed against a primary source before it carries a conclusion.

## Calendars

**Russian Empire.** Church registers used the Julian calendar. It ran 11 days behind the
Gregorian calendar until Julian 28 February 1800, 12 days behind from Julian 29 February 1800,
and 13 days behind from Julian 29 February 1900 (Gregorian 13 March 1900) until the 1918
switch. A January or February 1900 date is therefore still 12 days off.

**Soviet Russia.** The switch was made by decree: 31 January 1918 was followed by 14 February
1918. During the transition, old-style dates were often given in brackets beside the new
ones. A document from early 1918 may carry either, or both, so read the form of the entry
and note which calendar each date uses. Do not convert on sight; record the date as
written, name the calendar, and record the converted date alongside it.

**Habsburg Galicia and Bukovina.** The state used the Gregorian calendar. Orthodox registers,
and some Greek Catholic ones, may carry Julian or double dates. Treat each register's
calendar as unknown until the book itself shows it, for example from a feast-day note, a
dual date or a header.

## Borders and successor states

The record sits with the authority that governed the place on the date of the event.

- **Russian Empire to the Soviet Union.** The authority and the record kind both change
  around 1917-1918.
- **Galicia.** After 1918 Galicia went to Poland, after a short-lived West Ukrainian state
  had held its eastern part. Carpathian Ruthenia (not Galicia) went to Czechoslovakia.
- **Bukovina.** After 1918 Bukovina went to Romania.
- **1939-1940 and 1944.** Eastern Galicia and northern Bukovina came under Soviet rule in
  1939-1940 and again from 1944.
- **Civil registration.** Soviet civil registration replaced church registration from the
  decrees of December 1917 onward. It reached the territories annexed in 1939-1940 later
  than that.

Work out for each event which of these applied, and then which kind of institution would hold
the record now. A record created under one state is often held by the archive of another.

## Record kinds by case type

**Church registers, by confession.** Births, marriages and deaths were recorded by religious
body, so the confession decides which register to search. The person's confession can differ
from the family story. Not independently verified for this skill: entries often name godparents and
witnesses, which feeds the cluster analysis in phase 6.

**Revision lists.** Ten revision lists (tax and conscription enumerations) were taken from
1719 to 1858. Not independently verified for this skill: they list households with ages,
which lets you follow a family between register entries, but what each list recorded varied
between the ten.

**The 1897 census.** The empire's first and only general census. Not independently
verified for this skill: it records household members with relationships and ages, and what
survives is uneven by region, so a missing household is not proof of absence.

**Soviet civil registration.** From the decrees of December 1917. Not independently verified
for this skill: books may sit with a civil registration office, a state archive or a regional
archive, so ask which holds the year. Certificates issued to the family are derivative of the register unless the book itself
is seen.

**Military service and wartime losses.** Conscription lists, service records, casualty lists
and lists of the missing. A casualty list entry shows a person was listed; it is not an
account of what happened. Not independently verified for this skill: which of these survive
for a given period varies a great deal, so ask the holder what exists before concluding from
an absence.

**Repression and rehabilitation files.** Lists, case files and later rehabilitation records.
Treat them as sensitive findings: state what the record says in its own words, do not assert
a specific deed from a list entry, and read `privacy-and-sensitive-findings.md`. Not
independently verified for this skill: access to such files is usually restricted to
relatives and handled by a specific holder, and the rules differ by country and period.

**Evacuation.** Not independently verified for this skill: wartime evacuation left registers
and lists at the receiving places, in workplace files and in later claims, and a person
absent from the home town in those years may appear in a place the family never mentioned.

**Emigration.** Not independently verified for this skill: departure and arrival records,
passenger lists and naturalisation papers exist on both sides, and the spelling of a name
often changed at the border. Search both ends.

## Naming conventions

Not independently verified for this skill, but standard across the region:

- A patronymic derives from the father's given name ("son of" or "daughter of") and is used
  as a middle name. It gives you the father's given name from a single record, and it differs
  between siblings of different sexes.
- Many Slavic surnames have a masculine and a feminine form. A woman and her brother can
  appear under different endings, and a search on the masculine form misses her.
- Registers use the religious given name; the family used a diminutive or a local form.
  Search both, and note the equivalents in the name-variant sheet.
- A woman appears under her maiden name in her own baptism and in a parent's records, and
  under her husband's surname after marriage, sometimes with the maiden name added.
- The same name is transliterated differently by the Latin-script, Cyrillic and other-script
  records, and by the country that did the transliterating.

## Spelling before and after reform

Not independently verified for this skill: the Russian orthography was reformed around
1917-1918, removing several letters and changing endings, so a surname in a pre-reform
register can look different in print from the same name in a later document. Search with
both spellings, and with the letters that were dropped. Registers in other languages of the
region had their own spelling conventions, which also changed over time.

## Working order

1. Date the event, and name the state that governed the place that day.
2. Name the calendar the record is likely to use, and mark it unknown for Galicia and
   Bukovina until the book shows it.
3. Name the record kinds that state would have made, and the kind of institution that holds
   them now.
4. Fill the name-variant sheet before the first search.
5. Record dates as written, with the converted date beside them.
