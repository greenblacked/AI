# Weekly Review

A good weekly review ends with a short list of what happens next week, a shorter list of what consciously does not, and an honest sentence about why last week went the way it did.

Most personal review systems fail in one of two ways. The first is the inventory ritual: an hour spent emptying an inbox, filing notes and re-sorting a task list, which feels like control and changes nothing about Monday morning — every item still exists, none has been chosen over another, and the week ahead is identical to the one that would have happened without the review. The second is the archaeology project: three reviews are skipped, the fourth is attempted as a reconstruction of everything that has happened since, it takes ninety unpleasant minutes, and the unpleasantness guarantees the fifth is skipped too. Both are fixable by the same two commitments — the review produces decisions rather than tidiness, and it is short enough to survive a bad week.

## The one output

The review has exactly one output: **what you are doing next week, and what you are consciously not doing.** Everything else — the collected notes, the calendar scan, the reconciliation — is input to that decision. State this at the start of every session. A review that ends with a tidier system and no changed decision has not been run; it has been performed.

## Scope

Use for: the weekly review itself, planning the coming week against real capacity, reconciling last week honestly, restarting after missed weeks, pruning recurring commitments, and the monthly or quarterly look at whether the weeks add up to anything.

Do not use for: turning reading into durable notes, team-level delivery reviews or goal setting, career and job-search decisions, or nutrition, training and habit tracking.

## Timebox it

A shorter review that happens beats a thorough one that does not. Propose a shape and hold to it:

| Pass | Full review | The five-minute version |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Collect | 10 min | Skip; use what you remember plus the calendar |
| Reconcile | 5 min | One sentence: what did not happen, and why |
| Decide | 10 min | Three commitments, one of them the priority |
| Schedule | 5 min | Block the priority only |

Thirty minutes is the target for a full review; forty-five is the ceiling before it starts eating the thing it is supposed to protect. When the user is tired, late, or has already missed the slot, run the five-minute version and say so explicitly — the point is that the streak survives. The offer should be made rather than negotiated: "we have got fifteen minutes, so let us do the short one" is better than asking whether they would like to do less.

## Workflow: the four passes, in this order

Doing decide before collect plans around the work the user happens to remember, which is the work that shouted loudest, not the work that matters.

### 1. Collect

Sweep the places work actually accumulates. Ask the user which of these apply and go through them by name rather than saying "gather everything":

- Notes and scratch files from the week, including the ones opened and abandoned.
- Messages flagged, starred or left deliberately unread as a to-do.
- The calendar, both directions: last week for what really happened, next week for what is already committed.
- The task list, including whatever has been silently rolling over for a month.
- Physical and mental open loops — a form on the counter, a call not returned, a conversation being avoided, a decision waiting on someone else.

Capture into one list without deciding anything yet. Deciding while collecting means the first three items get careful thought and the rest get triaged by fatigue.

### 2. Reconcile against last week's plan

Put last week's commitments next to what happened, one line each, and name the gap's cause. This is the part people skip, and it is where the information is.

| What happened | Usual cause | What to change |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Not started, no interruption to blame | Avoidance, or the task is undefined | Name which. If undefined, the next commitment is to define it |
| Started, ran long | The estimate was wrong | Halve the next estimate for work of this type |
| Displaced by something urgent | Interruption pattern | Is it the same source every week? Then plan for it or fix it |
| Displaced by someone else's dependency | Capacity was never yours to spend | Move it to a waiting-on list; stop re-committing to it |
| Done, but not the thing that mattered | Priority was not set, or not protected | Set one priority next week and block it first |

The category worth pressing on is avoidance. Work that is not started, week after week, with no interruption to point at, is almost never a time problem — it is usually a fear of a conversation, an unclear first step, or a task the user has quietly decided not to do. People file this as "no time" for months. Naming it honestly is the single highest-value minute of the review. The resolution is one of three: shrink it to a first step small enough to be uncomfortable rather than frightening, delegate or ask for what is missing, or drop it deliberately and stop carrying it.

### 3. Decide

- **Start from the calendar, not the task list.** Count the hours genuinely free next week after meetings, commitments, commute, and recovery from whatever is heaviest. What remains is usually far less than a notional forty — for many people with a normal meeting load it is ten to fifteen hours of real focused capacity, and it is lower again in an on-call or travel week. Plan against that number.
- **Commit to a small number of things.** Three to five for a normal week, fewer when the calendar is full. More than about five and the list is a wish, and the reliable output of a wish list is guilt rather than work.
- **Make each one verifiable.** "Draft sent to Priya" and "migration runbook merged" can be checked on Friday. "Work on the migration" and "think about hiring" cannot, so they will neither be done nor honestly marked undone.
- **Name the one that matters most** — the thing that, if the week goes badly and only one thing survives, should be the survivor. Block it in the calendar now, at the time of day the user actually thinks well, before anything else claims the slot.
- **Write the not-doing list.** Two or three things being consciously deferred, with the reason. Undeclared deferrals come back as guilt every time the list is opened; declared ones are decisions.

### 4. Schedule

Put the commitments into the calendar as blocks with names, not as intentions. Then check the coming week for the things that need action *before* the next review: a deadline landing on Wednesday, a person to chase on Monday, a booking that expires. Finish by scanning what recurred: which meeting or standing commitment could be cancelled, shortened, or made asynchronous? Pruning one recurring commitment a month is the personal equivalent of killing a stale meeting series, and it is the only reliable way capacity ever increases.

## The longer cadence

A run of well-executed weeks can still be motion in no particular direction, which weekly reviews cannot detect because their horizon is seven days. Once a month, or once a quarter, ask four questions and write the answers down where next month's review will see them: what actually changed as a result of the last four weeks; which of the things carried every week are still uncompleted and should now be dropped rather than carried again; whether the current work moves anything the user says matters in a year; and what should stop. The monthly variant runs in fifteen minutes and does not replace that week's review — append it.

## Making it survive

- **A fixed slot,** same day, same time, in the calendar with a name. Friday afternoon suits people who want to close the week; Monday morning suits people who cannot think on a Friday. Either works; drifting does not.
- **A template that is filled, not composed.** Composing the structure each week is friction, and friction is what a weekly habit dies of. Use the format below verbatim.
- **A missed week is resumed, not reconstructed.** Start from now. Look back seven days at most, ignore the rest, and take five minutes to sweep for anything time-critical in the missed period. The instinct to catch up fully is what turned three missed weeks into six.
- **Adapt the shape to the person.** These passes are a starting point, not a law. Someone with a fragmented calendar may need two shorter reviews a week; someone on-call may need a version that plans only around the rotation; someone whose work is mostly reactive may find the not-doing list matters more than the commitments. Ask, after three or four weeks, which parts were useful and cut the rest — a system nobody has trimmed is one nobody is using.

Durable notes from things read or watched belong in `learning-notes`, not here. This review is planning; mixing knowledge capture into it is one of the ways the session quietly turns into an hour of filing.

## Standard output format: weekly review

```text
# Week of [date]

## Last week, honestly
- [committed] → [done / partial / not started] — [cause: avoidance, estimate,
  interruption, dependency, deprioritised]
- The gap that repeats: [pattern, or "none this week"]

## Capacity next week
Meetings and commitments: [hours] · Realistic focus time: [hours]
Known disruptions: [travel, on-call, deadline, personal]

## Commitments (3-5, each verifiable)
1. [specific, checkable outcome] — [when it is blocked]
2. [specific, checkable outcome]
3. [specific, checkable outcome]

**If only one thing survives:** [the priority] — blocked [day, time]

## Consciously not doing
- [thing] — [why, and when it gets reconsidered]

## Waiting on
- [person / thing] — [chase on which day]

## Prune
- [recurring commitment cancelled, shortened, or made async this week]
```

## Standard output format: monthly or quarterly review

```text
# [Month / quarter] review

## What actually changed
- [outcome, not activity]

## Carried every week and still not done
- [item] → [drop / shrink to a first step / schedule with a date]

## Direction check
The work of the last [period] moved: [the thing that matters], or: nothing much.
Evidence: [what a sceptic would accept]

## Stopping
- [commitment, project or recurring obligation being ended, and how]

## One change to the review itself
- [what to add, cut or shorten next month]
```

## Anti-patterns

- **A review that produces no decision.** An hour of filing, an empty inbox, and a Monday identical to the one that would have happened anyway. The output is the commitment list, not the tidy system.
- **Planning against a notional week.** Forty hours of plans against twelve free hours produces four missed commitments and a story about being undisciplined. Count the calendar first.
- **Reconstructing three missed weeks.** Archaeology is unpleasant enough to guarantee the next miss. Resume from today, sweep only for anything time-critical, and let the rest go.
- **A commitment you cannot verify.** "Work on the migration" is unfalsifiable, so it is neither finished nor honestly abandoned, and it rolls forward for a quarter.
- **Filing "no time" over avoidance.** The item untouched for six weeks with no interruption to blame is a decision the user has already made and not admitted. Name it, shrink it, or drop it.
- **Twelve priorities.** A list that long is a wish, and the week's real priorities get set by whoever emails first.
- **A system you spend more time maintaining than using.** Nested tags, custom dashboards and a weekly re-sort are procrastination with good posture. If the tooling costs more than the review, cut the tooling.
- **Reviewing without ever cancelling anything.** Commitments accumulate by default and only leave deliberately. A review that never removes a recurring obligation guarantees the calendar keeps shrinking.
