# Facilitating someone else's weekly review

Running the review as a conversation is different from handing over a template. The value
is in asking the questions the user would not ask themselves, and in stopping the session
turning into a comfortable inventory of everything that happened.

## Posture

Ask, then wait. The reviews that change anything are the ones where the user says the
awkward sentence out loud, and that only happens if there is room for it. Three rules:

- Do not supply the reason a thing did not happen. A guess ("sounds like you were busy")
  is usually wrong and always lets the user off the specific question.
- Do not congratulate a list. Praise for a full week of activity confirms motion as an
  achievement, which is the failure mode this whole practice exists to counter.
- Do not add items. The facilitator's job is subtraction and specificity, never a
  suggestion of one more thing that would be good to do.

## Questions that get honest answers

| Instead of | Ask |
| --- | --- |
| How was your week? | What did you say you would do, and what actually happened? |
| Why did that not get done? | What was in the way the first time you sat down to start it? |
| Are you happy with progress? | If nothing else had happened last week, would that one thing have moved? |
| What is your priority? | If Wednesday goes badly and everything after it is cancelled, what needs to be finished by Tuesday night? |
| Is that realistic? | Where in the calendar does that sit? Show me the block. |
| Anything to drop? | What has been on this list for a month, and what happens if you delete it? |
| How did you feel about it? | What would you do differently in the same week? |

Two more worth keeping: "what did you not do, that you told yourself you had no time
for?" and, at the end, "what did we decide?" — if the second cannot be answered in one
sentence, the review has not finished.

## Pressing on avoidance without lecturing

When an item has not moved for weeks and nothing external displaced it, name the pattern
neutrally and hand the diagnosis back:

> That is the third week for the pension transfer, and nothing seems to have landed on
> top of it. What happens when you get to it?

Then take the answer at face value. Typical answers and what they need:

| What they say | What it usually is | What helps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I do not know where to start | The task is undefined | Make the commitment "write the first three steps", not the task itself |
| I need to talk to X first | A conversation being avoided | Commit to sending one message, with a day and time |
| It is not really that important | Either true, or a defence | Ask what happens if it is deleted. If nothing, delete it |
| I keep running out of energy for it | Scheduled at the wrong time | Move it to the user's good hours and take something else out |
| I do not want to do it | Honest, and the most useful answer available | Delegate, shrink, buy the way out, or drop it deliberately |

Do not moralise, and do not repeat the point once it lands. One clear naming per session
is effective; two is nagging, and nagging is why people stop running reviews.

## Cutting twelve items to four

The list arrives long. Cut in this order, out loud:

1. Remove everything that is waiting on someone else. It is not a commitment; it is a
   chase with a day attached.
2. Remove anything that is not verifiable by Friday, unless it can be rewritten to be.
3. Ask which two items the user would be most annoyed to reach Friday without. Those stay.
4. Fit the rest to the capacity number. When the hours run out, the remaining items go on
   the not-doing list with a reason, not into an overflow section.
5. Read the final list back and ask whether the priority is right. It frequently is not,
   because the item that matters most is often the one that is least urgent.

## Adapting the cadence

The four passes are a starting point. Fit them to how the user's week actually goes:

- **On-call weeks.** Plan one commitment, not four, and put the not-doing list first.
  Reconciling an on-call week against a normal plan produces false evidence of failure.
- **Heavy travel.** Review on the outbound journey, plan in half-days rather than hours,
  and assume travel days produce nothing.
- **Mostly reactive work** (support, ops, management with an open door). Commitments
  compete with interruptions they cannot win. Shift the weight to two protected blocks
  and a serious prune, and measure the week by whether the blocks held.
- **Caring responsibilities or an unpredictable home week.** Plan against the worst
  plausible week, not the average one. A plan that only works if nothing goes wrong is
  the reliable source of guilt.
- **Fragmented calendars.** Two ten-minute reviews, Monday and Wednesday, beat one
  thirty-minute review that never survives the week.
- **A user who hates the format.** Ask which pass they skip when rushed; that pass is
  usually the one to cut permanently. A trimmed review that runs is worth more than a
  complete one that is abandoned in March.

## Signs the review is decaying

- The same three items roll forward every week and nobody mentions it.
- The not-doing list is empty, week after week — everything is still notionally on.
- Nothing has been cancelled or shortened in two months.
- The session runs long and ends without a decision anyone could state in a sentence.
- The user starts preparing for the review before the review, which means the review has
  become an audit they can fail.

Any two of these together are a signal to shorten the format rather than to try harder.
