# Escalation

Read this the moment a process misses its published response time, or a complaint is
rejected. Time limits at each stage are strict and unforgiving, and the specific windows
differ by country and sector — verify each one against the scheme's own page before relying
on it. What does not vary is the shape of the ladder.

## Why calling again does not work

A call costs you thirty to sixty minutes and costs the organisation a few pounds of
contact-centre time. It reaches someone with no history of your case, no authority to
decide it, and a script that ends at "I'll chase that for you". The asymmetry is the whole
problem: repeating the cheapest action for them is the most expensive action for you.

Every stage below does the opposite. It is written, it is logged against a reference, it
starts a clock that the organisation is measured on, and it moves the case toward someone
with authority.

## The ladder

| Stage | What it is | Typical clock | What it unlocks |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Written chase | A dated request for a decision or a decision date | Your own, usually 10 working days | Evidence you gave them the chance |
| Formal complaint | A separate, logged, timed process | Acknowledgement quickly, then a substantive response within the scheme's published window — eight weeks under the UK's FCA DISP rules, 15 days for an initial response under the US Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, 90 days for the body to conclude under the EU dispute-resolution directive | The internal escalation and the final response |
| Final response or deadlock letter | Their closing position in writing | Issued at the end of the complaint, or claimable when the window expires | The ticket to the external scheme |
| Ombudsman or regulator | A free external decision-maker | The scheme's own referral window: six months from the final response under UK FCA DISP, two years under Australia's AFCA | A binding or influential decision at no cost |
| Court or tribunal | A judicial determination | Limitation periods measured in years, but procedural deadlines in weeks | Enforcement |

Never skip the complaint stage. External schemes almost always refuse a case that has not
been through the organisation's internal process, and being refused on that ground wastes
the referral window.

The clocks above are examples, not a general rule: they are set by the scheme and differ by
country and by sector. Find the published window on the scheme's own page at the point you
open the complaint, and write that date at the top of the file. It is the number every
later stage is counted against.

## Stage 1: the written chase

One chase, in writing, quoting the reference, the date of receipt and the published
processing time. Ask for one of two things: the decision, or the date the decision will be
made and what is outstanding. Give a date, ten working days out, and state that the
complaints process follows.

Do not chase repeatedly. A second and third chase teach the organisation that chasing is
your only move.

## Stage 2: the formal complaint

The complaints process is a different channel from customer service, with different
staffing, its own reference, and published time limits the organisation is measured on —
in regulated sectors, by its regulator.

Make it unambiguous:

- Use the word **complaint** in the first line and in the subject.
- Send it to the complaints address published in the complaints procedure, which is usually
  not the address you have been using.
- State the facts in date order, the specific failure, the outcome you want, and the
  external scheme you will refer to.
- Ask for acknowledgement and the complaint reference.

Escalating within the complaint is normal: many procedures have two internal stages, and
the second is where a decision-maker with authority first reads the file.

## Stage 3: the final response or deadlock letter

The final response is the organisation's closing position. It should say what it found,
what it will do, and that it is the final response, and it usually has to tell you about
the external scheme and the time limit for using it.

Two things to do with it immediately. Diary the referral deadline the day it arrives — that
date is the one people miss. And if the complaint window has expired without a final
response, say so in writing and ask for a deadlock letter by name; in most schemes the
expiry of the window is itself enough to refer, with or without the letter.

## Stage 4: the ombudsman or regulator

An ombudsman scheme decides individual cases, is free to the consumer, and is generally
funded by the industry it oversees. A regulator supervises the sector and does not usually
resolve individual disputes, but a complaint to one affects the organisation's supervisory
record, which is a different kind of pressure.

What a referral needs:

- The chronology, one page, dated.
- The final response, or evidence the window expired.
- The submission pack and the correspondence.
- The outcome you are asking for, quantified.

What it is worth knowing before referring: the time limits are strict and expiry is
permanent; decisions can take months; accepting a decision usually binds you as well as
them, so read what acceptance forecloses; and the scheme can only decide what falls inside
its jurisdiction, which is defined by sector and sometimes by the size of the claim.

## Finding the right scheme

Ask three questions in order. Which sector regulates this activity — financial services,
energy, water, telecoms, transport, housing, health, legal or professional services, higher
education? Does the organisation belong to a named scheme, which its own complaints
procedure and website footer must usually disclose? And if there is no sector scheme, is
there a general consumer body, a trading standards equivalent, or an approved alternative
dispute resolution provider?

The organisation's final response is obliged in many sectors to name the scheme, which
makes it the fastest place to look.

## Parallel statutory routes

These are not part of the complaint ladder and are not paused by it. Run them on their own
deadlines, which are often shorter.

| Route | What it does | Watch |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Card chargeback | Reverses a card payment where goods or services were not provided as agreed | Scheme time limits are typically measured in months from the transaction or the expected delivery date, and are set by the card networks rather than by law. |
| Statutory liability of a credit provider | In some jurisdictions the lender is jointly liable with the retailer for purchases on credit above a threshold | Depends on how it was paid and the amount; check the local rule. |
| Regulator complaint | Affects the supervisory record | Does not compensate you individually. |
| Tribunal | Decides specific categories — employment, tax, immigration, property — cheaply and often without lawyers | Deadlines are short and strictly enforced; some are measured in weeks from the decision. |
| Small claim | A money judgment | See below. |

## Small claims

The small-claims procedure exists for people without lawyers: simplified rules, a modest
fee, and a track a litigant can run unrepresented. Where costs follow the event, as in
England and Wales, it adds the decisive feature of limited exposure to the other side's
legal costs if you lose.

Above the threshold the arithmetic changes, and the reason depends on the jurisdiction.
Under the English rule the losing party can be ordered to pay the winner's costs, which can
exceed the sum in dispute. In the United States the American rule applies instead: each
side bears its own attorney's fees regardless of the amount, in every state, absent a
statute or a contract term shifting them — so the exposure is not their bill but yours, for
representation a case at that size now needs and no judgment will give back. Either way,
the threshold is the point at which a professional stops being optional.

The thresholds differ by jurisdiction and by claim type, and they change. Check the current
figure for the relevant court before deciding which side of the line a claim sits on, and
check whether the claim type is excluded from the simplified track — personal injury and
housing disrepair often are.

Before issuing: check the limitation period, send a letter before action giving a deadline
and stating the claim and amount, and confirm you are suing the correct legal entity, which
is the registered company rather than the trading name on the shopfront.

## What escalation does not fix

- **A genuinely ineligible application.** No amount of escalation creates a qualifying
  condition. Find the alternative route instead.
- **A missed statutory deadline with no discretion.** Some are absolute.
- **A dispute of fact with no evidence on your side.** This is why the record-keeping comes
  first; escalation spends the evidence you kept, it does not create it.
- **Immigration, court proceedings and anything above the small-claims threshold.** These
  need a professional, and the right moment to get one is before the first mistake rather
  than after the third.
