# When it has already broken

Read this on the day the habit has broken, or when someone arrives having failed at the
same change three or four times. The work here is diagnosis rather than encouragement: a
collapse is evidence about the design, and restarting the same design harder produces the
same collapse on roughly the same schedule.

## The first five minutes

Do not open with reassurance and do not open with a plan. Ask three questions and write the
answers down.

1. When was the last time it happened, and what was different about that day?
2. What was the last thing you did before each of the last few misses?
3. If it never restarted, what would you actually lose?

The third question is the one that decides whether this is a design conversation at all.
Someone who cannot answer it is usually working on a goal that belongs to somebody else.

## Diagnosing from the miss pattern

Misses cluster. Find the cluster and the fault is usually obvious.

| Pattern | Almost always means | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Misses on the same weekday | The anchor does not exist on that day | A different anchor for that day, or an explicit rest day so it is not a miss |
| Misses when away from home | The design was tied to one physical context | A travel version, defined at half the floor, written before the next trip |
| Misses after a long day | The floor was set against a good day | Halve the floor; the current one is the target, not the floor |
| Misses started after one specific week | A life change removed the cue | Rebuild the anchor around the new routine rather than the old one |
| Never missed, then stopped completely | Streak-driven, and the first break took it all | Change the counter to days-this-month, restate that a floor day counts |
| Drifted without a clear break | The behaviour was doing nothing the person could feel | Reconnect it to an outcome, or drop it honestly |

## Design fault or unwanted goal

Some habits do not fail; they are quietly declined. Signals that this is the case: the
person keeps restarting in January or on Mondays, never chooses it over anything else, has
no answer to what they would lose, and describes it in terms of what they should do.

Say this plainly and offer the exit. Dropping a goal deliberately is a legitimate outcome
of this procedure and a better one than a fifth failed attempt, which costs real
self-trust. If the underlying want is real but the expression is wrong — they want to be
stronger rather than to run — redesign around the want.

## The restart

The restart is not the old plan with more resolve.

1. **Restart at the floor, for seven days, regardless of the previous level.** The old level
   is the reason the design broke; returning to it restarts the countdown to the same
   break.
2. **Change exactly one element.** The anchor, the floor size, or one environment step.
   Changing everything makes the next result uninterpretable.
3. **Do not backdate or make up for missed days.** There is nothing to repay. A doubled
   session to compensate reinforces the idea that a miss is a debt, which is the
   interpretation that ends attempts.
4. **Restate the recovery rule out loud, and shorten it.** After a collapse it usually needs
   to be smaller and blunter: one sentence, one action, no conditions.
5. **Set the next review at fourteen days**, and make the question "is the cue firing" rather
   than "how is it going".

## Repeated failure at the same change

Three or more attempts at the same behaviour means the design has never been the variable.
Work through these in order before attempting a fourth.

- **Has the floor ever been genuinely small?** In most repeat cases it has not — the person
  has restarted at the ambitious size every time, because the small version feels like
  giving up. That feeling is the fault being diagnosed.
- **Is there an anchor, or only a time?** Repeat failures are dominated by clock-based
  plans.
- **What does the behaviour cost that nobody has named?** Time taken from a partner,
  a shower and a change of clothes on a workday, a social cost, money. An unnamed cost is
  paid anyway and drives the quitting decision from outside the design.
- **Is somebody else's approval the motivator?** Those attempts end when the audience stops
  watching, which is reliably around week three.
- **Is there a physical or clinical obstacle?** Pain, untreated sleep problems, depression,
  ADHD, medication side effects. A behaviour design does not fix any of these and running
  one on top of an untreated condition manufactures another failure to add to the pile. Say
  so and point at the right kind of help.

## Disruptions you can see coming

Holidays, house moves, a new job, a newborn, surgery and travel all remove context cues
wholesale, and context is where much of the behaviour lives. Treat a known disruption as a
scheduled event with a written plan rather than a surprise.

- Define the travel or disruption version of the floor in advance, at roughly half.
- Name the new anchor for the new context, even if it is temporary.
- Decide in advance whether the habit is suspended rather than attempted — a deliberate,
  dated suspension with a named restart day is a decision and resumes cleanly; an
  undeclared one becomes a collapse with a story attached.
- Put the restart date in the calendar before the disruption starts, because nobody sets it
  afterwards.
