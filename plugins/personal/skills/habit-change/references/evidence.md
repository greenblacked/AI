# What the habit evidence actually says

Read this when someone questions a number, quotes the twenty-one-day figure, or asks how
much confidence a claim deserves. Each entry names the study, what it measured, and where
it stops being load-bearing. Claims are weaker than popular summaries of them, and saying
so is part of the job — a person who later discovers the number was oversold stops
trusting the rest of the design.

## Time to automaticity

Lally, van Jaarsveld, Potts and Wardle (2010), *European Journal of Social Psychology*.
Ninety-six participants chose one daily eating, drinking or activity behaviour, performed
it in a consistent context for 84 days, and rated automaticity daily on the Self-Report
Habit Index. The median time to reach the asymptote of the automaticity curve was 66 days,
with individual times from 18 to 254 days.

Know what that number is counted over, because it is the largest caveat on it. Ninety-six
people enrolled, 82 returned usable data, the asymptotic curve was fitted to 62 of those,
and 39 of the 62 fitted it well. The 66-day median and the 18-to-254-day range are
properties of that modelled subset, measured against 95% of each person's fitted asymptote
— not of the enrolled sample, and not of anyone who dropped out because it was not working.

What it supports: a review at day 66 rather than day 21, and a warning about variance
before day one. What it does not support: a promise that 66 days works for a given person,
or any claim at all about complex multi-step behaviours. The sample was small,
self-selected and the behaviours were simple and daily. Missing a single day did not
measurably affect the curve, which is the finding behind the never-twice rule — note that
the rule itself is a heuristic, not something the study tested.

## Implementation intentions

Gollwitzer and Sheeran (2006) meta-analysed 94 independent studies covering roughly 8,000
participants and found a medium-to-large effect, d around 0.65, for if-then plans
specifying when, where and how a goal-directed action would be performed, over and above
goal intentions alone. In plain terms, specifying the situation roughly doubles
follow-through in the kinds of study collected.

The mechanism is delegation of the start decision to the situation rather than to the
person, which is exactly what the anchor sentence in step 3 encodes. The effect is
strongest for behaviours people genuinely want and forget or postpone, and weakest where
the person is ambivalent about the goal itself.

## Context and repetition

Wood, Quinn and Kashy (2002) had participants record their behaviour hourly and found
roughly 43% of daily actions were performed in the same physical location almost daily.
Habitual behaviour was less affected by intentions and more by stable context.

Practical consequence: a person who moves house, changes jobs or goes on holiday loses
context cues wholesale, which is why habits break during disruptions and why that is a
predictable event to plan for rather than a personal failure. The same mechanism is an
opportunity — a move is the cheapest moment to install a new behaviour, because the old
cues are gone anyway.

## Friction and choice architecture

Rozin, Scott, Dingley, Urbanek, Jiang and Kaltenbach (2011) ran cafeteria field studies in
which small changes — moving a food item about 25 cm further along the counter, or
replacing a serving spoon with a smaller one — cut how much of it people took by 8 to 16%,
without any information, persuasion or restriction. The authors describe the effect as
modest but reliable, and that is the right size to plan around.

The general point is that a step of effort is a real intervention with a measurable size.
The general point is also the limit: these are small effects on single choices in one
setting, not evidence that layout beats every other factor. Use friction as the cheap first
move because it keeps working while the person is tired, not because it is stronger than
everything else.

## Lapses and relapse

Marlatt and Gordon's relapse-prevention model (1985) named the abstinence violation effect:
after a lapse, a person who reads it as proof of personal failure is markedly more likely
to abandon the attempt than one who reads it as a situational event. The lapse is small;
the interpretation is what decides the outcome.

This is the entire justification for writing the recovery rule on day one. The
interpretation has to be fixed while the person is calm, because the moment it is needed is
the moment they are least able to choose it well.

## Rewards, and what happens when they stop

Milkman and colleagues (2021) ran a megastudy with around 60,000 members of a gym chain,
testing 53 different four-week interventions against a control. Planning prompts and small
rewards for returning after a missed session produced the largest gains during the
intervention — and most effects decayed substantially after the intervention ended.

Read this as a caution about external motivators: they buy weeks, not habits. The design in
this skill spends its effort on the cue and the environment because those persist when
nobody is running a programme. Temptation bundling (Milkman, Minson and Volpp, 2014, at a
university gym) is worth using for its mechanism — it attaches the behaviour to something
the person independently wants, so nobody has to sponsor it — but it is not an exception to
the decay. In that study the gym-visit advantage faded over the Thanksgiving break and did
not recover. Recommend it because of how it works, not because it has been shown to last.

## Stopping versus reducing

Lindson-Hawley and colleagues (2016), *Annals of Internal Medicine*, randomised 697
smokers to abrupt cessation or two weeks of gradual reduction, both with support and
nicotine replacement. Abstinence at four weeks was 49% for abrupt against 39% for gradual,
and the gap persisted at six months. Notably, participants who preferred gradual reduction
still did better when assigned to quit abruptly.

The transferable claim is narrow but useful: for a behaviour with a strong cue and a
supply, a stop date beats a slope, and the person's own preference for tapering is not
evidence that tapering will work for them.

Wegner, Schneider, Carter and White (1987), *Paradoxical Effects of Thought Suppression*,
supply the other half: people instructed not to think of a white bear reported it more,
both while suppressing and afterwards. Wegner (1994), *Ironic Processes of Mental Control*,
named the mechanism — suppression runs a monitor that searches for the thought, and the
search is what keeps supplying it. Which is why stopping designs work on cues, supply and
substitution rather than on resisting urges.

## Where the twenty-one days came from

Maxwell Maltz, a plastic surgeon, wrote in *Psycho-Cybernetics* (1960) that it seemed to
take a minimum of about 21 days for patients to adjust to a changed face. That observation
about post-surgical adjustment became, through decades of retelling, a law about habits.
There was no study, no measurement of automaticity and no habit formation involved. Saying
this plainly matters, because a person expecting automaticity at three weeks and not
getting it frequently concludes the problem is them.
