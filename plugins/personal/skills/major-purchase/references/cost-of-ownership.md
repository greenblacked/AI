## How to build a total

Fix the holding period first; nothing below means anything without it. Then sum purchase
price, minus expected resale, plus every recurring cost multiplied by the years, plus every
one-off cost inside the period. Divide by the years for the per-year figure, and by the
units of use — miles, hours, loads, cycles — where options differ in how much use they can
absorb.

Keep the arithmetic in one place and label every cell known or assumed. The value of the
exercise is mostly in discovering which single assumption the ranking depends on.

## Vehicles

Depreciation, insurance, fuel or electricity, road tax, servicing at the manufacturer
interval, tyres, brakes, a battery on an internal-combustion car and eventually on an
electric one, a roadworthiness test each year after the exemption period, breakdown cover,
parking or permit, zone and congestion charges, cleaning, and finance interest.

The figures that surprise people: tyres are a set every 25,000-40,000 miles and a set on a
larger wheel size costs double a small one; insurance varies by more than a factor of two
between models in the same price band, so quote the specific model rather than the class;
and an electric car trades lower running and servicing costs against a steeper early
depreciation in most current markets, which is why the comparison has to be over the whole
holding period rather than per mile.

For a lease or contract plan, the total is deposit plus every payment plus any final fee,
plus excess mileage at the contract rate for realistic annual distance, plus expected
end-of-contract condition charges. Compare that to purchase price minus expected resale over
the same term.

## Property

Transaction tax, legal and conveyancing, survey, mortgage arrangement fee, mortgage interest
over the holding period, buildings and contents insurance, service charge and ground rent on
a leasehold, local property tax, maintenance, utilities if they differ materially from the
current home, moving costs both ways, and selling costs.

Maintenance is the component most often set to zero and it should be roughly 1% of property
value a year averaged over a decade, higher on anything built before modern insulation and
wiring standards. The large items are not annual: a roof, a boiler, windows and a rewire
each land once every fifteen to thirty years in four or five figures, and averaging them is
the only way they are ever budgeted.

Transaction costs across a buy and a later sell commonly total 5-10% of value, which sets
the break-even horizon against renting. Compute that break-even explicitly; it is usually
between three and seven years and it is the number that decides most buy-or-rent questions.

## Computing and phones

Purchase price, an operating system or software subscription the device requires, storage
subscription, accessories that are genuinely required rather than wanted, battery
replacement, an extended warranty only where the repair cost is both high and likely, and
resale value at the end.

Service life is set by security-update support rather than by hardware failure. Check the
published support window before comparing prices; a device with two years of updates left is
a two-year purchase whatever its condition. Non-upgradeable memory and storage make the
initial specification a permanent one, which shifts the right answer towards buying more
memory than is needed today and less storage, since storage can be added externally and
memory cannot.

## Large appliances

Purchase price, delivery and installation, disposal of the old unit, energy over the
expected life at the local tariff, water where metered, consumables such as filters and
descaler, repair probability, and the realistic service life.

Energy is the component that reverses rankings. A machine used daily for ten years can
consume several times its purchase price in electricity, so an efficiency difference that
looks marginal on the label is a large number over the life. Compute energy across the
holding period explicitly and put it in the table.

Repairability matters more than warranty length: check spare-parts availability and the
typical cost of the two most common failures for the model. An unrepairable appliance has a
service life equal to its warranty, whatever the build quality.

## Courses and qualifications

Fees, materials, examination and registration fees, travel and accommodation, software or
equipment required, renewal or continuing-education costs to keep the qualification valid,
and the income forgone during study — which is usually the largest component and the one
never counted.

Value it against a named outcome: a specific role, a specific rate change, or a specific
capability the user can point at. A course bought for general improvement with no named
outcome cannot be compared to anything, which is why the comparison is usually skipped for
exactly the purchases where it matters most.

## Depreciation shapes

Use these when no market data is available, and replace them with real listing prices the
moment those can be found. They are shapes, not forecasts.

| Category | Year 1 | Year 3 cumulative | Year 5 cumulative | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| New mass-market car | 20-30% | 40-50% | 55-65% | Varies most with brand and fuel type; check listings for the same model at target age. |
| One-to-three-year-old car | 10-15% a year | — | — | The flatter part of the curve, which is the whole argument for buying here. |
| Laptop or phone | 25-40% | 55-70% | near-zero resale | Cliff-edged at the end of software support rather than gradual. |
| Large appliance | steep and irrelevant | — | — | Second-hand market is thin; assume no resale and plan on service life instead. |
| Quality bicycle, tool or instrument | 20-30% | 35-45% | 40-50% | Then nearly flat, sometimes rising for sought-after items. |
| Furniture | 50-70% | — | — | Mass-market furniture has effectively no resale; solid-wood and designer pieces behave like the row above. |

## When a figure cannot be obtained

Put a range on it, use the midpoint in the total, and state the range next to the total. Then
test whether the ranking changes at each end of the range. If it does not, the unknown does
not matter and the decision can proceed; if it does, that unknown is the next thing to find
out and everything else can wait. This is a faster route to a decision than researching
every component evenly.
