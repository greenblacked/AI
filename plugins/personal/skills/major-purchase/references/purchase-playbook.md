# Purchase playbook

Read this before a showroom visit, a property viewing, or any negotiation. It carries the
pre-commitment template, the negotiation sequence, the questions to ask at the point of
sale, and the add-ons to refuse by name.

## The pre-commitment template

Write this before any contact with a seller, and read it back before signing anything.

```text
# Pre-commitment: [item]        written [date]

Requirement this satisfies: [one sentence]
Holding period: [years]

Maximum all-in price: [amount]
  — includes delivery, installation, first-year insurance, taxes and every fee
Specification agreed: [list]
Explicitly excluded: [add-ons by name]

Walk away if:
  1. [condition — a price, a finding, a missing document]
  2. [condition]
  3. [condition]

Pause: at least 24 hours between best offer and acceptance. No exceptions for
       a deadline offered by the seller.
Decision deadline: [date] — after which pick on reversal cost and commit.
Also has to agree: [who]
```

The two clauses that do the work are the all-in maximum, which stops the price being moved
by adding items rather than by raising the headline, and the pause, which is the only
defence against urgency that survives being in the room.

## Negotiating on total price

Sequence, in order. Each step is cheap and each one is skipped more often than it is used.

1. **Know the target before arriving.** Completed sale prices, several sellers, a valuation
   guide. The target is a number, not a range.
2. **Negotiate the cash price first, alone.** Keep finance, trade-in and add-ons off the
   table entirely until the cash price is agreed. A seller working across all four at once
   can give on one and take more on the others, which is the whole reason they are bundled.
3. **Then ask the finance question both ways.** "What is the cash price, and what is the cash
   price if I take your finance?" A gap between the two is a real cost of the finance and
   should be converted to an effective rate before the 0% is believed.
4. **Then the trade-in, separately.** Get an independent valuation and a competing offer
   first. A generous trade-in allowance funded by a smaller discount on the purchase is the
   oldest version of this trade.
5. **Ask for the total out-the-door figure in writing** before agreeing to anything. Fees
   appear at this step that were not mentioned at any earlier one.
6. **Be willing to leave, and say so early and pleasantly.** The single most effective
   negotiating position is a written maximum and a genuine alternative, and both exist
   already if steps 1 and 5 of the workflow were done.

Silence is a tool. After a price is stated, saying nothing is more effective than any
counter-argument, and costs nothing.

## Questions at the point of sale

Ask these, and write the answers down:

- What is the total out-the-door price, itemised, including every fee?
- What is the total repayable under the finance, and the APR?
- What exactly is covered by the warranty, for how long, and what voids it?
- What is the return or cancellation window, and does it apply to an in-person purchase as
  well as an online one?
- What are the two most common repairs on this model, and what do they cost?
- Is the delivery or completion date contractual or an estimate?
- What happens if it arrives damaged or faulty, and who arranges collection?

A seller who cannot answer the warranty and return questions precisely is telling you
something about what happens if the item is faulty.

## Used items: what to verify before money moves

- **Ownership and finance.** Confirm the seller owns it outright and nothing is secured on
  it. On a vehicle this is a specific check and it is the one that can cost the buyer both
  the item and the money.
- **History against the record.** Service history, mileage or usage consistency, previous
  damage, any write-off or insurance record.
- **Independent inspection.** On anything mechanical above a few thousand in value, from
  someone with no relationship to the seller. The fee is a small fraction of the repair it
  finds, and a clean report is also a negotiating position.
- **Identity and serial numbers.** Match the numbers on the item to the paperwork. A
  mismatch ends the transaction.
- **Payment route.** Paying at least part of the price by a method that carries buyer
  protection is worth its fee in several jurisdictions, because it makes the lender jointly
  liable for a faulty good. Check whether that applies locally.
- **Viewing conditions.** Daylight, dry weather, cold engine. Each of these hides a
  different defect when absent, and a seller insisting on an evening viewing in the rain has
  chosen the conditions.

## Add-ons, and which two are worth a second look

Refuse by default: paint and fabric protection, most extended warranties, service plans
priced above the sum of the services, cable and accessory bundles, delivery insurance on a
carrier that is already liable, and any protection product explained for the first time at
the counter.

Two deserve an independent look, priced away from the point of sale where they are
consistently cheaper:

- **An extended warranty** where the likely failure is both expensive and probable — a
  complex appliance with a known failure mode, or an item whose repair cost approaches its
  replacement cost. Compare the premium to the repair cost multiplied by a realistic
  failure probability. Most fail this test; a few pass it clearly.
- **Shortfall or gap cover** on a financed item that depreciates faster than the loan
  amortises, where a total loss would leave a debt and no item. Price it from an independent
  provider rather than the seller.

## After the purchase

Register the warranty and diarise its expiry. Put the annual running costs into the
irregular-but-certain bucket of the budget rather than discovering them next year. Keep the
receipt, the specification and the pre-commitment together, so that the next purchase in the
same category starts from what was actually learned rather than from memory. And record the
one thing that turned out differently from the model — the insurance quote, the maintenance
bill, the resale price — because a total-cost model that is never checked against outcomes
stays wrong in the same direction every time.
