# Decision traps

Read this at step 6, or whenever a decision keeps reversing. Each entry gives how the trap
sounds in conversation, why it works, and the counter-question that defuses it.

## Anchoring

**Sounds like**: "It was listed at 32,000 and they came down to 27,500." "This one is
expensive but the top model is 40% more."

**Why it works**: the first number seen sets the scale for every number after it, including
numbers the buyer knows are arbitrary. Sellers exploit it deliberately with a high list
price, a deleted "was" price, and a premium option positioned to make the middle option feel
moderate.

**Counter**: establish the target price from independent sources — completed sales, multiple
sellers, a valuation guide — before looking at any one seller's price, and write it into the
pre-commitment. Then judge the offer against the target, never against the list.

## Sunk cost on research

**Sounds like**: "I have spent three weekends reading about this, I am not starting again."
"I have already put a deposit down on the survey."

**Why it works**: effort already spent feels like an asset that would be destroyed by
changing course, when it is gone under either choice.

**Counter**: ask what someone arriving fresh today, with the same requirement and the same
budget but no history, would choose. If the answer differs from the current plan, the
research is not informing the decision, it is defending it. Note that a survey or inspection
fee that reveals a problem has already delivered its value — the discovery is the product.

## Specifying for the rare case

**Sounds like**: "I need seven seats for the two times a year my parents visit." "I want the
tow bar in case I ever get a trailer." "The bigger machine in case I edit video."

**Why it works**: the rare case is vivid and the premium is spread invisibly across every
day of ownership.

**Counter**: take the frequency number from step 1 and price the alternative — hiring,
borrowing, a taxi, a delivery, paying someone else. A vehicle hire for two weekends a year
costs a fraction of the permanent premium for a larger vehicle, and the arithmetic is rarely
close. Where the rare case is genuinely non-negotiable, keep it, but keep it as a stated
cost rather than an assumption.

## The discount as a reason

**Sounds like**: "It is half price." "This deal is too good to pass up."

**Why it works**: a saving is framed as a gain, and gains do not feel like spending.

**Counter**: ask whether this option would be bought at full price if the requirement is
met. If not, the discount is buying a thing that was not needed, and the money spent is a
loss of 100% of the price paid rather than a saving of the difference. Decide the option
first, then the price.

## Monthly payment framing

**Sounds like**: "It is only 280 a month." "What monthly payment were you hoping for?"

**Why it works**: the monthly figure can be set to any value by extending the term, so it
carries no information about cost at all while feeling like the most concrete number in the
room.

**Counter**: ask for cash price, total repayable and APR, and refuse to discuss the monthly
figure until all three are on the table. A seller who will not state the total repayable has
answered the question.

## Manufactured urgency

**Sounds like**: "This price is only available today." "There is someone else looking at it
this afternoon." "The offer ends at the weekend."

**Why it works**: it compresses the interval between wanting and committing, which is the
interval in which every counter in this file operates.

**Counter**: the mandatory 24-hour pause in the pre-commitment, applied without exception.
Genuine scarcity exists — a specific used item really can be sold to someone else — but the
correct response is to accept losing it sometimes rather than to remove the pause. Losing
one item is cheap; a rushed decision on a multi-year purchase is not.

## Specification creep at the point of sale

**Sounds like**: paint protection, fabric protection, an extended warranty, a service plan,
gap insurance, a cable bundle, an installation upgrade.

**Why it works**: these are offered at the moment commitment is highest and attention is
lowest, are individually small against the purchase price, and carry the highest margin in
the transaction.

**Counter**: name the excluded add-ons in the pre-commitment before arriving, and treat any
of them as a separate decision to be made after the pause, not as part of this one. Two are
worth a genuine second look on their merits — an extended warranty where the likely repair is
both expensive and probable, and gap cover on a financed vehicle that would leave a shortfall
if written off. Price both independently rather than at the counter, where they are
consistently more expensive.

## Identity purchases

**Sounds like**: "This is the kind of thing a serious cyclist buys." "I want something that
says I have my life together."

**Why it works**: the purchase is doing a job, just not the one written in the requirement,
and the real job is never stated so it is never priced.

**Counter**: this is legitimate and should be kept — but on its own line, with its own
number. Say what the premium above the capability-meeting option is, and let the user decide
whether that is worth paying. People are usually comfortable paying it once it is named, and
uncomfortable only when it is disguised as a requirement.

## Analysis as avoidance

**Sounds like**: a fourth week of comparison between two options that are within a few per
cent on every axis.

**Why it works**: research feels like progress and carries no risk of being wrong.

**Counter**: when the options are close, the decision is cheap and more research will not
separate them. Pick on reversal cost — which is easier to sell, return or replace — and
commit. State a decision deadline in the pre-commitment for exactly this reason.

## Running the checklist without arguing

Work through the traps as questions rather than accusations. "How many times a year will that
happen?" and "what would the total repayable be over three years instead of five?" do the
work without requiring the user to concede anything. A trap named as a mistake gets defended;
a trap surfaced as a number gets recalculated.
