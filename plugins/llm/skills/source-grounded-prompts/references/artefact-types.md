# Other artefact types

Read this at step 4 when the artefact is not a deck. The grounding block, the accessibility block and the checker work the same way for all of them. What changes is what replaces the layout catalogue, and which cap does the job the per-slide word cap does for a deck.

## Briefing memo

A briefing memo is read once, usually by someone deciding something.

- Replaces the layout catalogue with a **section order**: the decision or conclusion first, then the evidence, then what is uncertain, then what is asked of the reader. Offer the order as the default and let a section be dropped when the sources hold nothing for it.
- Caps: a total length in words, a paragraph cap of about five sentences, and a rule that every paragraph opens with its point.
- Grounding: each claim carries a bracketed source and section. A figure is stated once, with its unit and period, as the source states it.
- Avoid: a summary that repeats the body, recommendations the sources do not support, hedging in every sentence.

## Study guide

A study guide is for someone who will be tested or must apply the material.

- Replaces the layout catalogue with three structures: **questions** (retrieval prompts, with the answer on a separate line that names its source and section), a **glossary** (a term, one sentence in the sources' own meaning, and where it is defined), and **worked examples** (a problem taken from the sources, each step shown, never an invented one).
- Caps: a number of questions per section, a glossary limited to terms the sources use, one worked example per concept at most.
- Grounding: a question the sources cannot answer is not asked. A worked example whose numbers are not in the sources is a placeholder figure and is removed.
- Avoid: trick questions, a glossary of common words, answers that quote a long passage instead of stating the point.

## Audio-overview script

An audio overview is heard, so structure has to be carried by the voice.

- Replaces the layout catalogue with a **segment plan**: an opening that says what the listener will learn, two to four content segments, and a close that restates the single takeaway. Offer the plan as a default.
- Caps: a **turn-length cap**, such as about 40 words per speaker turn, and a cap on how many numbers one turn may carry (two is a suggested default, so a listener is not asked to hold more; raise it if the audience is expert); a rule that a number is spoken with its unit and said again in the next turn only if it matters.
- Register: state who speaks, how they address each other, and that spoken text contains no markup, no "as shown on the slide", and no spelling-out of citations; source names are spoken naturally ("the second report") and listed after the script.
- Grounding: quotations are marked in speech ("the author writes") and are exact; a figure not in the sources is left out rather than rounded.
- Avoid: filler openers, laughter cues, a turn that is a list, any claim of certainty stronger than the sources state.

## Mind map

A mind map is a tree, and its failure is depth and sprawl.

- Replaces the layout catalogue with a **depth limit** and a **branch rule**: at most three levels below the centre, three to seven branches at the first level, and a node label of at most six words.
- Structure: the centre is the concept line; each first-level branch is one theme the sources develop; deeper nodes are claims or terms, each grounded.
- Grounding: a node carries a source reference when it states a claim; a label that only groups other nodes needs none. Cross-links between branches are allowed only where a source draws the connection.
- Output: state a plain indented-text form, since the tool may render the tree itself; no colours carry meaning on their own.
- Avoid: a node that is a full sentence, a branch with a single child, a fourth level.
