# Promise, proof or pressure

Contents: the three classes and their tests, questions that surface a contingency, weighing a competitor move, and handling an executive request.

## The classes

| Class | What it looks like | Evidence that qualifies | What it earns |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Promise | Already committed in writing | A contract clause, an order form, a dated public commitment, a signed statement of work | Delivery as committed, or an explicit renegotiation with the customer |
| Proof | Delay has a measured cost | A signed contingency; deals lost with this as the recorded reason; churn with this as the stated cause; a dated regulatory requirement | A place in the trade-off, priced by the evidence |
| Pressure | Urgency without either | Conviction, a single call, a forecast, a competitor announcement, "everyone is asking" | A smaller version or a dated answer, rarely a slip |

Pressure is not illegitimate. An executive's conviction can be right. It means the decision is a bet, and the log should say so, so that it can be scored later.

## Testing a promise

Find the document. A verbal promise by a salesperson may or may not bind the company, depending on the contract (for example an entire-agreement clause) and the jurisdiction, so have legal confirm before treating it as a commitment; until then record it as pressure with a note on who made it, and fix the sales process separately. Check whether the promise has a date. A promise without a date can usually be met with a dated commitment rather than a slip.

## Testing claimed revenue

Revenue counts only when the customer has put the condition in writing. Ask the account owner:

- Is there an order form or renewal held pending this? Who signed it, and what date does it say?
- If not, will the customer's signatory put in writing that they will sign if it is delivered by a date?
- What else is in the way of the deal: price, security review, a competing vendor, budget timing?
- What will the customer do if we never build it? (A customer who has a workaround and will renew anyway is not a contingency.)

If the answer to the second question is no, the revenue is pressure, whatever the forecast says. Record the forecast figure beside the signed figure, so the gap is visible to the decider.

## Weighing a competitor move

A competitor's launch is pressure until your own data says otherwise. Check:

- Deals in the last two quarters where the competitor won and this capability was the recorded reason, from the CRM's loss-reason field or win-loss interviews.
- Churned or downgraded accounts that named it.
- Open deals where the prospect has raised it in writing.

If the loss-reason field is empty or "other" for most deals, that is a finding in its own right: the organisation cannot currently tell whether the competitor matters, and the cheapest next step is to ask the last five lost prospects.

## Handling an executive request

Executives ask for things with less context than the team has, and the team has less context than the executive about the business. The ledger is how the two meet. Bring it with the three options; do not bring a refusal. If the executive chooses the full build after seeing what slips, that is a decision made properly, and the log records it as theirs.

Questions that clarify an executive ask without arguing with it:

- What happens if this ships in the next quarter instead?
- Who outside the company is expecting it, and have they been promised a date?
- Of the items in the ledger, which would you move first?
