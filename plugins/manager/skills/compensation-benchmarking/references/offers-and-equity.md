# Offers and Equity

Read this when pricing an offer, setting counter-offer policy, or running the pay-equity
check: how to construct an offer inside a band, the equity component and its mechanics,
the counter-offer as a retention decision, the cohort method for equity analysis and its
traps, and a worked merit-matrix allocation.

## Constructing an offer

An offer is a placement plus a story. Decide all four before the number goes out.

1. **The level**, fixed by the ladder, not by what the candidate asks for.
2. **The position in the band.** A standard offer sits at or just above midpoint. Above
   midpoint is a deliberate decision about scarcity or a hot skill, recorded as an
   exception.
3. **The mix.** Base, target bonus and equity, stated separately. A candidate comparing
   total comp to your base will conclude you are behind when you may not be.
4. **The reason.** One line a stranger could read: "mid-band for the level; above
   midpoint for the specialised stream-processing background we have been unable to hire."

Where the candidate's expectation is above the band, the choice is an exception with a
record, or a no that names the band as the reason. What you do not do is quietly move the
band, which sets the ceiling for the next hire without a decision.

## Equity mechanics

For options and RSUs, four numbers decide the real value, and candidates rarely check all
four.

| Number | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| Grant size | Shares or units, not a currency figure whose value is the company's own optimistic estimate |
| Strike price | For options, the exercise cost; a low strike is worth more than a large grant with a high one |
| Vesting schedule | Four years with a one-year cliff is standard; a longer cliff is a retention lever and a risk |
| Valuation basis | The 409A or last-round price the grant was struck against, and how old it is |

Refresh grants matter for retention: a person four years in has vested everything and is
being paid below market on the equity line, which is a compression problem wearing a
different hat. Model equity as a range with the assumptions stated, never as a single
number presented as certain.

## The counter-offer decision

A counter-offer is a retention decision, and the first question is not "how much" but
"what changed". If the honest answer is "nothing, we are afraid to lose them", that is a
retention case: fund it as one, with its own reason, and fix the underlying cause —
usually pay that fell behind the band or a level that was never recognised.

Set policy in advance, because deciding case by case under pressure is how a structure
erodes.

- **Match only what the band supports.** A counter above the band is a band change; make
  it deliberately or not at all.
- **Never counter without asking why they looked.** The external offer is rarely the whole
  reason, and a counter that ignores the real reason buys a few months.
- **Say what the counter is contingent on**, if anything, and put it in writing.
- **Expect the team to learn the lesson you teach.** If outside offers are the only path
  to a raise, you will get more outside offers.

## The pay-equity check by cohort

Aggregate pay gaps hide cohort gaps. Compare like with like, at three levels of rigour.

1. **Group means, by level and role.** The starting signal. A gap here is a prompt, not a
   conclusion.
2. **Ratio against the reference group**, per cohort: median pay of the group of interest
   divided by median pay of the reference group, at the same level, role family and
   location. Report the cohort size beside every ratio.
3. **Regression controlling for level, tenure and performance.** The defensible version:
   it isolates the residual gap that the controls do not explain. This is the number to
   act on, and the one to bring to a budget owner.

Traps that produce a wrong answer:

- **Small samples.** A cohort of six cannot support a percentage; report it as
  inconclusive rather than as a finding.
- **Explaining the gap away with performance** when performance is not in the model. If it
  belongs in the model, put it in and show the residual.
- **Controlling for the outcome.** Do not control for the current salary or the current
  level if those are themselves the thing under examination.
- **One year's data.** A gap can be a hiring-cycle artefact; look across two or three
  cycles before concluding, and act sooner on any cohort that is both large and large-gapped.
- **Fix the structure, not only the people.** If new hires of one group consistently enter
  lower in the band, the offer process is the cause, and repricing individuals will not
  hold.

## The merit matrix

Allocate the cycle budget with a written matrix before the calibration room convenes. A
common shape weights performance against position in the band, so a strong performer low
in their band moves most and someone at the ceiling moves only on promotion.

| Performance | Low in band | Mid band | High in band |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Exceeds | 7% | 5% | 3% |
| Meets | 5% | 3% | 1% |
| Below | 0% | 0% | 0% |

Worked allocation, a £40,000 merit pool across twelve engineers:

| Group | People | Avg salary | Matrix rate | Cost |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Exceeds, low/mid band | 3 | £85,000 | 6% | £15,300 |
| Meets, mid band | 6 | £92,000 | 3% | £16,560 |
| Meets, high band | 2 | £110,000 | 1% | £2,200 |
| Below | 1 | £80,000 | 0% | £0 |
| **Total** | **12** | | | **£34,060** |

The pool leaves £5,940 for the equity fixes and the compression repricing identified in
step 6 of the skill. If there is not enough for both, that trade-off goes to the budget
owner with both numbers, rather than being resolved silently in the room. Every deviation
from the matrix carries a written reason; the deviation with no reason is the one that
reappears next cycle as a compression problem.
