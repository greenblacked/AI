# Scenarios

Read this when the conversation is one of the named types. Each entry gives the outcome
worth aiming at, an opening you can say out loud, the trap specific to that scenario, and
what closing it looks like. The workflow in `SKILL.md` is unchanged; these are the
adjustments that type makes to it.

## Contents

- Underperformance
- Refusing a request, including from your own manager
- An unpopular decision or reorganisation

## Underperformance

**Outcome.** The person knows which standard is being missed, in specific terms, knows what
changed behaviour looks like, and has a date when it is looked at again.

**Opening.** "I want to talk about the quality of the last two projects. I don't think the
work is at the level we need, and I want to be specific about what I mean and hear where
you disagree."

**Trap.** Softening it into a growth conversation. If you leave and they believe it was a
development chat, you have created the surprise you will be accused of at the next stage.
The tell is a meeting in which the word "standard" never appears.

**Adjustments.** Say early whether this is a normal management conversation or the start of
something formal, and do not leave that ambiguous — people can tell the difference and will
assume the worse one if you dodge. Involve HR before, not after, the first formal step, and
follow the local process; employment law varies by jurisdiction. Name your own share
honestly where it exists, because unclear expectations are the most common cause and you
will not fix the performance without fixing that.

**Close.** A specific standard, a specific date, a specific check-in, and a written note the
same day.

## Refusing a request, including from your own manager

**Outcome.** The no is understood as final, the reasoning is visible, and an alternative is
on the table.

**Opening.** "I can't take the migration on alongside the launch. I want to walk you
through what would have to give, and then suggest two options."

**Trap.** Hedging into a maybe. A no delivered as "I'll try" costs more than a clean no,
because it consumes the other person's planning as well as your time, and it will be
remembered as a commitment.

**Adjustments.** Refuse on the constraint rather than on preference: capacity, sequencing,
risk, or a commitment already made. Bring the trade-off explicitly — what you would stop
doing in order to say yes — and let them choose. Where the answer is genuinely theirs to
overrule, say so and take the decision cleanly rather than relitigating it afterwards.

**Close.** Which option was chosen, what is now not being done, and who tells the people
affected.

## An unpopular decision or reorganisation

**Outcome.** They understand what has been decided, why, what it means for them
specifically, and what is still open.

**Opening.** "I've made a decision about how the team is structured, and it affects you
directly. I'll explain the decision and how I got to it, then I want to talk about what it
means for your work."

**Trap.** Inviting input on the part that is closed. The moment you ask "what do you
think?" about a fixed outcome, you have promised something you cannot deliver. Ask about
implementation, sequencing and support, which are real.

**Adjustments.** Say the decision in the first sentence, not after the reasoning; people
stop listening to reasoning while waiting to hear the outcome. Be precise about what is
still open and give it away generously, because it is the only genuine agency on offer.
Expect the reaction to arrive a day later rather than in the room.

**Close.** What changes, when, who else knows, and when you will next talk.

## Telling someone they did not get the role

**Outcome.** They know the outcome, the reason in terms of the role rather than their
worth, and what would change the answer next time — if anything would.

**Opening.** "I want to tell you before it is announced: the lead role has gone to Ana. I
want to be straight with you about why, and about what I think your path looks like."

**Trap.** Implying a next time that does not exist. If the honest answer is that this role
is not on their path here, softening it costs them a year. If there genuinely is a path,
name the specific gap and the assignment that would close it.

**Adjustments.** Tell them before the announcement, and never in the same conversation as
the announcement to everyone else. Expect the second conversation — the real one usually
happens a few days later once it has landed, so book it.

**Close.** The specific gap or the honest absence of one, and a date to talk again.

## Bad news that is nobody's fault

Project cancelled, funding cut, a favourite piece of work reassigned, a relocation.

**Outcome.** They hear it from you first, they understand what is fixed, and they know what
support exists.

**Opening.** "I've got news that I know will be unwelcome, and I wanted you to hear it from
me first. The Atlas programme is being stopped at the end of the month."

**Trap.** Padding it with context before the news. Every sentence before the news is
experienced as evasion once the news lands, and it is the part people replay.

**Adjustments.** Lead with the fact, then the reasoning, then what happens next. Do not
pretend to more certainty than you have about second-order effects — "I don't know yet, and
I will tell you on Thursday" is stronger than a reassurance that turns out to be wrong.
Where you disagreed with the decision, say that you carry it rather than performing
distance from it.

**Close.** What is decided, what is not yet, and when they will hear the rest.

## Behaviour towards other people

**Outcome.** They know exactly which behaviour, in which moment, and what its effect was on
a named piece of work or on people's participation.

**Opening.** "In Thursday's review I saw something I want to talk about, and it is my own
observation rather than something passed to me."

**Trap.** Aggregated anonymous complaint. "A few people have found you difficult" cannot be
checked, cannot be acted on, and turns a work problem into a hunt for who talked. Raise
what you saw, or get the source's agreement to be named.

**Adjustments.** Keep to behaviour and effect, never to personality; character words land
differently depending on who the person is, which is exactly why they are the words that
appear in discrimination claims. For anything that may be harassment or discrimination,
stop running this as a feedback conversation and take it to the formal route immediately.

**Close.** The specific behaviour that changes, and what you will be watching.

## A peer who is not your report

**Outcome.** The working problem is named and there is an agreement, without a request for
authority you do not have.

**Opening.** "Something in how our teams are working together is costing us, and I'd rather
raise it with you than route it upwards. Can I describe what I'm seeing?"

**Trap.** Escalating first. A complaint that reaches your peer through their manager makes
every subsequent conversation with them a defensive one, and it is remembered long after
the issue is resolved.

**Adjustments.** Frame it entirely as a shared cost to the work, and ask for their reading
before offering yours — you have less context on their constraints than you think, and
peers are usually operating under one you cannot see. If it does not resolve after a second
attempt, say plainly that you are going to raise it with both managers, and do that rather
than surprising them with it.

**Close.** One agreed change, one date to check, and both of you telling your own teams the
same thing.
