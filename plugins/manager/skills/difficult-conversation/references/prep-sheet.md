# The prep sheet

Read this in the hours before the meeting. Twenty minutes of writing here is what keeps the
conversation from being improvised, and improvisation is where the softening sentence and
the buried point come from. Write it on paper or in a scratch file; do not compose it in
your head, because the rehearsal in your head casts the other person as an agreeable actor.

## The sheet

1. **The one outcome.** One sentence, observable. What must be true when this ends.
2. **Type.** Feedback, where the conclusion is genuinely open, or a decision already made,
   where delivery and support are open and the outcome is not. Write which.
3. **The instances.** Two or three, with dates. Situation, behaviour a camera would have
   recorded, impact that followed. Most important first.
4. **The story column.** What you have concluded about their intent. Written down
   deliberately so it stays out of the room, or gets offered as an inference rather than
   smuggled in as a fact.
5. **The ask.** The observable behaviour you want instead. If you cannot write it as
   something a person does on Monday, you do not yet know what you are asking for.
6. **My contribution.** What you did, or failed to do, that is part of the situation.
   Unclear expectations, two quarters with no feedback, a shifting priority you never
   restated. Naming it first costs nothing and buys most of the room's temperature.
7. **Their strongest case.** Their side in its strongest form, in their words rather than a
   weakened version you can beat. If you cannot write it, you are not ready.
8. **The opening, verbatim.** Two sentences. Topic, type, why you are raising it, stop.
9. **Support offered.** What changes on your side.
10. **The floor.** The minimum acceptable outcome, and what you do if you do not get it.
11. **Reaction plan.** One line each for the two reactions you think are most likely.

## The questions that expose an undecided outcome

Ask these of your own sheet before the meeting. Each one catches a common failure.

- Would this conversation have succeeded if they disagreed with me at the end? If no, this
  is a decision and calling it feedback will be found out.
- What would I change about my position based on what they say? If nothing, write that
  down and say it in the room.
- Is any of this new to them? If yes, the first sentence has to concede that I should have
  said it sooner.
- Can I state the ask as something observable? "Be more proactive" is not an ask.
- What am I afraid they will say? That is usually the true thing I have not prepared for.
- Am I raising this because it affects the work, or because it irritates me? Both are
  legitimate to feel; only the first is legitimate to raise as feedback, and dressing the
  second as the first is transparent.
- If this goes well, what do I do on Friday? A conversation with no follow-through teaches
  that the topic was survivable by waiting.

## Worked example: three missed deadlines

**One outcome.** Daniel understands that committing to a date and announcing the slip on
the day is the specific problem, and we have agreed how he flags risk earlier, with a
check-in on Friday.

**Type.** Feedback. The conclusion is open — if the cause is a dependency I did not clear,
the ask changes.

**Instances.**

- 4 March, sprint commitment on the search index. Told the team it would not land on the
  morning of the review. Two people had scheduled work behind it and lost the day.
- 18 March, same pattern on the reindex script. QA had held a window open.
- 8 April, migration doc committed for the Friday, raised as not done in Monday's standup.
  The dependent team planned their sprint around it.

**Story column.** He has stopped caring about commitments; he is avoiding hard work. Stays
out of the room — the pattern is also consistent with him discovering problems late, or
with him being afraid to raise a slip early.

**The ask.** When a commitment looks at risk, say so at the point it looks at risk, not on
the due date — in the standup or directly to me, whichever comes first.

**My contribution.** I never said what I wanted from a slipping commitment, and in January
I reacted badly the one time someone flagged a risk early. He may be reading that.

**Their strongest case.** Every one of those three had a genuine dependency outside his
control, and he has been carrying interview load I asked him to take on.

**Opening, verbatim.** "I want to talk about how the last three sprint commitments have
gone — specifically the timing of when we found out they would slip. This is feedback and I
want your read on it, because I think some of it is mine."

**Support.** Interview load reduced for the next month if that is the constraint. A
standing ten minutes on Wednesdays to surface risk before the review.

**Floor.** Agreement on when risk gets raised, and one specific mechanism. If nothing
changes by the next sprint review, the conversation becomes explicit about the standard and
what happens if it is not met.

**Reaction plan.** Counter-accusation about the interview load — concede immediately, it is
true, then return to the topic. Flat agreement with no detail — ask him to say what he will
do differently and what will be hard about it.

## What to do with the sheet afterwards

Keep it. Add what actually happened, in two lines, the same day you send the follow-up
note: what they said, and what was agreed. This is the record that makes the next
conversation continuous rather than a fresh start, and if a formal process ever follows, a
contemporaneous note is what makes the earlier conversation real. Do not circulate it; it
contains your story column, which was never meant to leave the page.
