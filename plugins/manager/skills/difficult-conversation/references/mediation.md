# Mediating between two people

Read this when the conversation is between two other people rather than with one of them.
Mediation is a different procedure from feedback: you are not delivering a message, you are
running a meeting in which two people say things to each other that they have so far only
said to you. The main failure is holding the joint session too early, which converts a
workable disagreement into a rehearsed one.

## Decide whether to mediate at all

Mediate when two people who need each other's work have a pattern of friction and both
still want it to work. Do not mediate in three cases, because mediation makes each of them
worse:

- **A performance problem wearing a conflict costume.** If one person's work is the issue,
  the joint session invites the other to prosecute it. Handle it as underperformance with
  the individual.
- **Harassment, discrimination, bullying, or anything with a power difference running
  through it.** Asking someone to sit down with a person who has mistreated them is harmful
  and, in most organisations, a process violation. Route it formally.
- **A structural conflict.** Two teams with incompatible objectives will keep producing
  friction between whoever holds those objectives. Interpersonal mediation on a structural
  problem is how the same meeting happens every quarter with different people in it. Fix
  the objectives.

Say which of these you have concluded, out loud, to the person who brought it to you.

## The separate conversations first

Half an hour each, before any joint session, in whichever order does not signal a side.

Ask each of them: what is happening, in specific instances rather than in general; what
they think the other person believes about them; what they want that they are not getting;
what their own contribution has been; and what a good outcome would look like in practice.
The fourth question is the one that decides whether a joint session is viable — a person
who cannot name any contribution of their own will use the joint session to prosecute.

Two things to get from each of them before you convene anything. First, agreement to the
joint session and to what is on its agenda; an ambush produces a defended position rather
than a conversation. Second, one thing each is prepared to change, secured in private, so
the joint session starts with something to build on rather than with two demands.

Do not carry messages between them. A mediator who relays becomes the channel, and the two
of them stop needing to speak at all.

## The joint session

Forty-five minutes, a room with a door, you facilitating rather than participating.

1. **State the purpose and the rules in two sentences.** The purpose is a working agreement,
   not a verdict on the past. The rules: one person speaks at a time, describe behaviour and
   effect rather than character, and nothing said here is repeated outside the room.
2. **Each describes the working problem for three minutes, uninterrupted.** Behaviour and
   effect only. Time it visibly; the limit is what stops the first speaker from setting a
   frame the second spends the session fighting.
3. **Each says back what they heard**, before responding to it. This is the step people try
   to skip and the one that does most of the work — it is usually the first time either has
   heard their position stated by the other without a rebuttal attached.
4. **Name the shared cost.** The delayed release, the rework, the thing neither of them
   wants. Both of them are for this, which makes it the only ground they start on.
5. **Trade one concrete change each**, the ones you secured privately. Specific, observable,
   small enough to happen this week.
6. **Write it down in the room** and read it back before anyone leaves.

Interrupt on character language, every time, and redirect to the instance: "what did he
actually do in that meeting?" Allowing one unchallenged "he's just difficult" sets the
register for the rest of the session.

If it escalates beyond recovery, stop it — do not push through. "We are not getting
anywhere useful today; I am going to stop here and we will pick this up tomorrow" costs one
day and preserves the possibility of a second attempt.

## Agreements that hold

Written, specific, and about behaviour rather than attitude. "Ana will post the schema
change to the channel before merging; Tom will raise objections there within a day rather
than in the review" holds. "They will communicate better" does not, because nobody can tell
on Friday whether it happened.

Send the agreement to both, same day, same text, from you. Set a check-in in two weeks with
both of them, and hold it even if things seem fine — an unchecked agreement decays, and the
check-in is also what makes the next escalation legitimate. Tell them explicitly what you
will do if the agreement does not hold, so the next step is not a surprise.

## When to stop mediating and decide

Mediation has a limit, and running past it is a failure of management rather than
persistence. Stop and decide when any of these hold:

- Two rounds have produced agreements and neither held.
- One person is engaging in good faith and the other is not, which you will usually see
  first in who keeps their private commitment.
- The cost to everyone else — meetings rerouted around the pair, people choosing who to ask
  — is now larger than the cost of a decision.
- The disagreement is about a technical or priority question that someone simply has to
  settle. Mediating a decision that needs an owner is how a two-week argument becomes a
  two-month one; make the call, write down the reasoning, and move.

When you decide, say plainly that mediation is over and that you are deciding, give the
reasoning to both at the same time, and be explicit about what happens next — including, if
it comes to it, that one of them will move teams. A decision taken late and clearly is
recoverable; a mediation nobody ended is what people leave over.
