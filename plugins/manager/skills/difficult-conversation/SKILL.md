# Difficult Conversation

The conversation is finished when the other person can say back, in their own words, what you asked for and what happens next, and a written note saying the same thing is in their inbox the same day.

These conversations go wrong in seven repeatable ways: postponed until the behaviour is months old; opened with a warm-up that buries the point; led with the story about intent rather than the behaviour observed; improvised, though the first sentence does more work than the next twenty minutes; the silence after the opening filled by the person who opened; a decision already made dressed as a discussion; and closed on a warm feeling rather than a specific next step. Each is worked through in the anti-patterns at the end, which is where the reasoning lives. This skill fixes the order: one outcome, observation separated from story, the honest label on what kind of conversation this is, two sentences written verbatim, situation-behaviour-impact, silence held, a named next step, and a written follow-up.

## Scope

Use for: preparing and holding the conversation itself — underperformance, a missed standard, a boundary or a refusal, a decision someone will not like, a conflict between two people you manage, news that lands badly, and the rehearsal of what to say when it goes sideways.

Do not use for: the written performance review, the rating, the levelling argument or the promotion packet — all of those are `growth-review`, which owns the document while this skill owns the meeting. Telling customers about an outage is `incident-comms`. Interview loops, scorecards and hiring debriefs are `hiring-loop`. A new joiner's first weeks and their early expectations are `onboarding-plan`. A blameless incident write-up is `postmortem`; if an incident conversation has turned into a performance conversation, that is a separate meeting and it is this one.

## Feedback or a decision already made

Decide this before anything is written, and say it in the opening sentence. The two conversations look similar and are not.

| | Feedback | A decision already made |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What is genuinely open | What happens next, and sometimes whether your reading is right | How it is implemented, what support comes with it, and the timing |
| The opening says | "I want to talk about X and hear your side of it" | "I have decided X. I want to explain how I got there and then talk about what happens next" |
| What their objection can change | The conclusion | The delivery, not the outcome |
| The failure | Softening it into a chat, so nothing is heard as feedback | Asking for input you will not use |

Dressing a decision as a discussion is the most damaging move available. The person argues their case, believes they were heard, discovers the outcome was fixed, and thereafter treats every consultation you run as theatre — including the ones that were real. If the decision is made, say it is made. People accept unwelcome decisions far better than they accept discovering they were performed at.

## Workflow

### 1. Name the one outcome

Write one sentence: what must be true when this conversation ends. One outcome, not three. "She understands that the missed deadlines have to stop and has agreed a check-in on Friday" is an outcome. "She takes it well" is a hope, and it is the hope that turns the conversation into a softening exercise.

Then ask yourself the calibrating question: would this conversation have succeeded if the person disagreed with you at the end of it? If the honest answer is no, you are not seeking understanding, you are seeking compliance — which may be legitimate, and if so it belongs in the decision column above rather than being dressed as feedback.

State what you are willing to change as a result of what you hear. If the answer is nothing, that is the honest label for the meeting.

### 2. Separate the observation from the story

Write two columns on paper before you write any script. On the left, only what a camera would have recorded. On the right, what you concluded from it.

| What I observed | The story I built |
| --- | --- |
| Three of the last four sprint commitments slipped, each announced on the day it was due | He has stopped caring about the team's commitments |
| She interrupted two people in Thursday's design review and the quieter of them did not speak again | She is dismissive of people more junior than her |
| The migration doc has been at 60% for five weeks | He is avoiding the hard part |

The left column is arguable on facts and can be checked. The right column is an inference about someone's inner life, and it is both the part most likely to be wrong and the part that triggers the defence. Take only the left column into the room, and if the inference matters, offer it as an inference: "the story I find myself telling is that the team's commitments have stopped mattering — tell me where that is wrong." Stone, Patton and Heen's *Difficult Conversations* (1999) makes the mechanism explicit: we judge ourselves by intent and others by impact, so an accusation of intent is nearly always answered with an account of intent, and the conversation never reaches the behaviour.

### 3. Prepare on paper

Twenty minutes of writing, done at least a few hours before. Rehearsing in your head produces a version in which the other person says the lines you gave them.

Write down: the one outcome; two or three specific instances with dates and what happened, not a general impression; what you are asking for, stated as an observable behaviour; what support you are offering; the strongest version of their side of it, in their words; your own contribution to the situation, stated plainly, because it is usually real and naming it first costs you nothing and buys a great deal; the minimum you will accept; and what you will do if you do not get it. `references/prep-sheet.md` is the one-page version with a worked example — read it while preparing rather than trying to hold this list in your head.

Check two things before the meeting exists. First, whether this is genuinely new to them: a surprise in a difficult conversation is a management failure regardless of the content, and if it is the first they have heard of it, that is the first thing you say. Second, whether HR, a formal process, or employment-law constraints apply — in most jurisdictions the point to involve them is before the first formal conversation, not after it.

### 4. Set it up so the room is not the first shock

Book it directly and briefly, the same day or next: "I'd like half an hour tomorrow to talk about how the Atlas work is going." Do not send "we need to talk" on a Friday evening; you buy yourself a weekend of their anxiety and a defensive person on Monday.

In person or on video with cameras on, never over chat, and never in a room with a glass wall onto the team. Half an hour, with nothing immediately after it, so neither of you is managing a clock. If the person is likely to want support, say in advance that they can bring someone; in some jurisdictions and for some formal processes, they are entitled to.

### 5. Write the first two sentences verbatim

The opening decides the rest. Carrère and Gottman (1999), *Predicting Divorce among Newlyweds from the First Three Minutes of a Marital Conflict Discussion*, Family Process 38(3):293-301, coded the first three minutes of a conflict discussion in 124 newlywed couples and predicted, from that alone, which marriages ended in divorce across a six-year follow-up; a harsh start-up was what separated them. Read it for what it is: marital-conflict research applied here by analogy, it says nothing directly about a manager and a report, and this literature has been criticised for fitting its models after the outcomes were known. The practice it supports is still the right one, because the first three minutes are the part you can decide in advance and the only part you get one attempt at. Write them down and read them if you have to.

A good opening does four things in two sentences: names the topic plainly, says what kind of conversation this is, says why you are raising it, and stops. No warm-up, no weather, no sandwich.

| Do not open with | Open with |
| --- | --- |
| "How was your weekend? Great. So, look, I wanted to pick up on a few small things…" | "I want to talk about the last three sprint commitments. This is feedback, and I want your read on it as well as mine." |
| "I don't know how to say this, so I'll just say it…" | "I have made a decision about the team structure and you are affected. I'll explain how I got there, and then I want to talk about what it means for you." |
| "Everyone has been saying that…" | "In Thursday's design review I saw something I want to talk about, and it is my own observation." |
| "You've been really negative lately." | "In the last two retros you opened with what would not work before anyone had described the option. Here is the effect that had." |
| "I'm worried you're not coping." | "The migration doc has been at 60% for five weeks. What is in the way?" |
| "This is probably nothing, but…" | "This matters enough that I want half an hour on it." |

The sandwich — praise, criticism, praise — is worth naming as a defect rather than a style. It teaches people that praise precedes bad news, so the praise stops being usable, and it lets the message be filed under the reassurance on either side of it. Where the praise is true, say it on a different day.

### 6. Deliver situation, behaviour, impact

The Center for Creative Leadership's SBI model is the unit, and it works because each part answers the objection the previous one invites.

- **Situation** — where and when, specifically. "In Thursday's architecture review" can be checked; "sometimes in meetings" cannot, and the argument becomes about whether it is sometimes.
- **Behaviour** — what a camera would have recorded. "You cut across Sam twice and finished his sentence the second time", not "you dominated".
- **Impact** — what followed, for the work or for people, stated as consequence rather than verdict. "Sam did not raise anything else in that meeting, and the option he was describing is the one we are now rebuilding."

Worked, end to end: *"In Thursday's architecture review, you cut across Sam twice and finished his sentence the second time. He didn't raise anything else in that meeting, and the option he was describing is the one we've now spent two weeks rebuilding. I don't think that was your intention — that's why I'm raising it."*

Two or three instances, most important first. A list of nine is an indictment, and the person will defend the weakest item on it and treat that as having answered all nine.

### 7. Stop talking

After the opening and the first SBI, say nothing. The silence will feel much longer to you than to them, and the instinct to fill it produces the softening sentence that deletes the message — "but obviously it's not a big deal" undoes the previous minute entirely.

Count to ten in your head if that is what it takes. Mary Budd Rowe's classroom research on wait time found that extending the pause after a question from about one second to three seconds dramatically lengthened and improved the responses; the same mechanism applies here, and the pause you need is longer than that because the content is harder. If they say nothing after a genuine silence, ask one open question and stop again: "what is your read on it?"

Then listen for long enough to be able to say their position back in a sentence they would accept. Do that out loud before you respond to it. It is the cheapest way to establish that this is not a verdict being read out, and it frequently surfaces the thing you did not know.

## When it goes badly

Plan for the reaction before the meeting; it is the part improvisation handles worst. In every row, the move is to slow down rather than to win.

| What happens | What is going on | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tears | Usually shame or exhaustion rather than manipulation, and it is rarely about the sentence you just said | Pause, offer a tissue and a break, and say you are in no hurry. Do not retract the message to stop the crying; you would be trading their dignity for your comfort and you will have to hold the conversation again anyway. Resume, or reconvene later that day if they cannot continue |
| Anger, raised voice | Threat response to status or fairness — David Rock's SCARF framing is a useful checklist for which one you tripped | Stay level and slow. Name it once: "I can see this has landed badly. I want to hear it." If it becomes personal abuse, end the meeting explicitly and reconvene tomorrow; do not negotiate under it |
| Flat denial | Either your evidence is thinner than you think, or they genuinely did not see it | Go back to the camera-level facts, one instance, and ask what they remember of that meeting. If your facts do not survive that, say so and stop; being wrong in public here costs far less than defending a bad claim |
| Counter-accusation about you | Sometimes deflection, often true and stored up | Concede what is true, immediately and without conditions, then say you will come back to it properly and return to the topic. "You're right that I didn't give you that context in March. I'll pick that up separately — and I still need to talk about the last three deadlines" |
| Going silent, shutting down | Overload, or a decision that engaging is unsafe | Stop delivering. Ask one question, wait, and if nothing comes, state the next step and the follow-up in writing and offer to continue tomorrow. Continuing to talk into a shutdown only adds to what they have to process |
| "Who said this about me?" | Reasonable, and the answer must be decided beforehand | Say what you observed yourself and own it as yours. If it is second-hand, either do not raise it or have the source's agreement to attribute it. Anonymous aggregated complaint is unusable as feedback and lands as a conspiracy |
| Agreement that is too fast | Compliance to end the meeting, not understanding | Ask them to say back what they will do differently. If it comes back as your words repeated, it has not landed — ask what will be hard about it |

If you are the one becoming angry, say you want ten minutes and take them. A conversation you lose your temper in becomes the only thing anyone remembers about the issue.

## Closing

Do not close on relief. Close on three things, out loud, in this order:

1. **The specific next step**, with an owner and a date. "You'll re-plan the migration with Priya and we'll look at it together on Friday at 2." Not "let's keep an eye on it."
2. **What happens at the check-in** — what you will be looking at, and what the consequence is if nothing has changed. State the consequence plainly if there is one. Withholding it to be kind removes the person's ability to make an informed decision about their own situation.
3. **Their words back.** Ask them to summarise what they are taking away. Do this even when it feels redundant, because it is the only point where you can find out that the two of you heard different meetings.

Then write it, the same day. A short note, sent to them, with no new content in it — anything not said aloud does not belong in the follow-up. Say what was discussed, what was agreed, the date, and what happens next. Offer them the chance to correct it, which protects them and is also how you find out what they actually heard. Copy nobody unless the process requires it and they know you are doing it.

The written follow-up is not paperwork against them. It is what stops the two accounts of this meeting from diverging over the next six weeks, and if a formal process ever follows, a contemporaneous note is what makes the earlier conversation real.

## Output format

Produce the prep sheet before the meeting and the note after it.

```markdown
## Prep — [name], [date]

**The one outcome:** [what must be true at the end]
**Type:** [feedback — the conclusion is open] or [decision made — delivery is open]
**Would this have succeeded if they disagreed?** [yes / no, and what that tells me]

### Observed / story
| What I observed | The story I built |
| --- | --- |
| [camera-level, with a date] | [the inference — stays out of the room unless offered as one] |

### The instances (2-3, most important first)
1. Situation: [when and where] · Behaviour: [what a camera saw] · Impact: [what followed]

### The ask
[The observable behaviour I am asking for]

### My contribution
[What I did or failed to do that is part of this]

### Their strongest case
[Their side, in its strongest form, in their words]

### Opening — verbatim
[Two sentences. Topic, type, why, stop.]

### Lines I will not cross
[Minimum acceptable outcome · what I do if I do not get it]
```

```markdown
## Follow-up note — sent [date]

Thanks for talking this through today. Writing down what I took away so we
are working from the same account.

**What we discussed:** [one or two sentences, no new content]
**What we agreed:** [the specific next step, owner, date]
**What I am doing:** [support, context, the thing I said I would fix]
**Next check-in:** [date, and what we will look at]

If any of that does not match what you heard, tell me and I will correct it.
```

## Anti-patterns

**The feedback sandwich.** Praise, criticism, praise teaches people that praise is a warning shot, so your genuine praise stops working, and it lets the message be filed under the reassurance around it. Deliver the message on its own and give the praise on a different day, when it is true and unattached.

**A decision dressed as a discussion.** You get an argument you were never going to lose, they get the discovery that they were performed at, and every real consultation you run afterwards is read as theatre. Say "this is decided" and spend the meeting on implementation and support.

**Leading with the story.** "You've checked out" is an inference about someone's inner life, so they defend their inner life and the behaviour is never discussed. Lead with what a camera recorded and offer the inference, if at all, as yours to be corrected.

**Filling the silence.** The softening sentence you add to relieve your own discomfort — "but it's really not a big deal" — deletes the minute before it, and you will hold the whole conversation again in six weeks. Count to ten.

**The nine-item list.** Saving up months of small instances produces an indictment; the person argues the weakest item and treats that as having answered all of them. Two or three, most important first, raised close to when they happened.

**Postponing until it is formal.** Every month of delay makes the first honest sentence harder, because it now also concedes that you watched it happen and said nothing — and it removes the person's chance to fix something while fixing it was still cheap.

**Second-hand feedback with a hidden source.** "Some people have said" is unusable — it cannot be checked, cannot be acted on, and converts a work problem into a hunt for who talked. Raise what you saw yourself, or get the source's agreement to be named.

**Retracting under tears or anger.** Softening the message to stop the reaction trades the other person's dignity for your comfort, and you will have to hold the meeting again anyway with less credibility. Pause, offer a break, hold the content.

**Closing on a good feeling.** Both people leave warm, with different accounts of what was agreed, and the same conversation happens again next quarter. Close on an owner, a date and their summary in their own words.

**No written note.** Without one the two accounts diverge quietly, and in any process that follows, a conversation with no contemporaneous record did not happen. Send it the same day, with nothing in it that was not said aloud.
