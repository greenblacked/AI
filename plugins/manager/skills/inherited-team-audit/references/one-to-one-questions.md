# One-to-one questions

Send the five questions the day before, in writing, in the same wording to everyone. Allow forty-five minutes to an hour. Ask for the person's account and do not defend, explain or promise.

## The five questions, with follow-ups

| Question | Follow-ups when the first answer is thin |
| --- | --- |
| What is working well that I should not break? | What would you miss if it went away? Who deserves credit for it? |
| What is broken or slowing you down? | What happened last time it bit you? How long has it been like this? Has anyone tried to fix it? |
| What would you stop doing if you could? | What does it cost per week? Who asked for it? Who would notice if it stopped? |
| What do you need from me? | What would make the next month easier? Is there a decision waiting on a manager? |
| Is there a promise, arrangement or deadline I may not know about? | Who was it made to? Is there someone I should speak to before changing anything near it? |

Add two standing prompts for the end: "What have I not asked that I should have?" and "Who else should I talk to?".

## Running the meeting

- Start by repeating the freeze: you are listening, not deciding.
- Take notes openly. Say what you will do with them (a themed summary, not attributed quotes).
- Do not ask people to assess colleagues. If they volunteer a view, note it as theirs and do not repeat it.
- Ask for specifics: an incident, a date, a document. A story is more useful than a general complaint.
- End by saying what happens next: the findings memo, the date, and how they will see it.

## Note template

```markdown
Name:                         Date:
Role and tenure:
Working well:
Broken or slowing:
Would stop:
Needs from me:
Promises or arrangements I may not know about:
Specifics worth checking (documents, incidents, dates):
Commitments mentioned (to whom, by when):
Risks mentioned (systems, people, expiries):
Follow-ups I owe them:
```

After the meeting, send the person a few lines of what you heard and ask them to correct it. Aggregate across all the notes before drawing conclusions; a theme counts when several people raise it separately.
