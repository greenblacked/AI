# Stay Interviews

Read this before running a stay interview: who should run it and when, the question set,
how to respond to a happy, an ambivalent and a ready-to-leave answer, and the follow-through
that keeps the next one honest.

## What a stay interview is for

A stay interview finds out what keeps someone and what would make them leave, while there
is still time to act on the answer. It is the preventive half of the exit interview, and
the only retention tool that works before the decision is made.

It is not a performance conversation, not a negotiation, and not a check that the person
is happy. The output is what the manager now knows and will change, and what the person
now knows about their own future here.

## Who runs it, and when

- **The person's own manager**, usually — the relationship is the thing most likely to
  cause a departure, and the conversation is part of managing. For a manager-of-managers,
  a skip-level occasionally is worth more, because people tell a skip-level what they will
  not tell their manager.
- **Not HR, and not as a form.** A stay interview run as a process produces process
  answers. It is a conversation with a purpose.
- **At a deliberate moment**, not only when trouble is suspected: a work anniversary, a
  manager change, the end of a large project, a promotion decision, or when a signal
  appears. Running it only under suspicion makes it a warning, and the person answers
  accordingly.

Tell the person in advance what it is and that nothing here is a formal record of a
problem. A surprise "how are you finding it" with a notebook reads as the start of
something else.

## The question set

Ask open questions, then be quiet long enough for the second answer. The first is the
rehearsed one.

1. What do you want to be doing in a year, and can you see it happening here?
2. What part of the job gives you energy, and what drains it?
3. What would have to change for you to still be here in two years?
4. When was the last time you thought about leaving, and what prompted it?
5. What are you learning, and is it enough?
6. Is there anything you have raised before that did not change? (The one that tests
   whether previous answers mattered.)
7. What do you want more of, and less of?

## Reading the three answers

| Answer | What it usually means | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Happy, with specifics | Genuinely engaged, and the specifics are the thing to protect | Note what keeps them; do not take it for granted |
| Ambivalent, or "fine" | A concern they are not yet naming, or a real drift | Ask the follow-up; watch the signals; do not accept "fine" |
| Ready to leave, or nearly | The decision is close | Ask what would change it, decide honestly whether you can offer it, and do not promise what you cannot deliver |

"Fine" is the answer to worry about. It is often the polite version of "I have stopped
telling you things", and it is worth one more question rather than a satisfied nod.

## The follow-through

The value of a stay interview is entirely in what happens after it. A conversation that
asked directly and changed nothing is worse than no conversation: it has taught the person
that even when asked, nothing moves.

- Act on one or two things, visibly, within a few weeks.
- Where something cannot change, say so plainly and say why, rather than leaving it
  ambiguous.
- Keep the promise about what happens next: if the answer was "we will look at the level
  in the next cycle", look at it in the next cycle.
- Follow up on the same topics in the next one-to-one, so the person sees the thread and
  knows the answers were kept.

If the honest outcome is that you cannot offer what the person needs, that is a finding
too — better learned now, with time to plan, than at the resignation.
