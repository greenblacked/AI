# Attrition Review

Read this when analysing departures: the fixed reason categories, the questions that get
past the polite answer, how to tell the stated reason from the probable one, the cohort
cuts that reveal a cluster, and a worked year of aggregated attrition.

## In this file

- Fixed reason categories
- Questions past the polite answer
- Stated versus probable reasons
- The cuts that reveal a cluster
- A worked year

## Fixed reason categories

Use the same categories every time, so the counts are comparable across years and across
managers. Twelve is enough; more fragments the pattern.

| Category | What it covers |
| --- | --- |
| Compensation | Pay below market, a band that fell behind, a level that was never recognised |
| Growth | No next step, a stalled promotion, skills outgrowing the role |
| Manager | The relationship or the management, named or implied |
| Team and peers | Conflict, isolation, a team that stopped working |
| Work content | The work itself — boring, unchallenging, or a direction the person disagreed with |
| Flexibility | Remote, hours, location, a life change the role could not accommodate |
| Company direction | Strategy, a reorg, a product decision, viability |
| Role change | A different role or career the current one could not offer |
| Return to study | Education, a break |
| Health or personal | The person's own reasons, at the level of detail they chose to give |
| Relocation | A move, theirs or a partner's |
| Involuntary or managed | Not retention data; keep it separate |
| Unknown | No data, or the person declined; count it rather than guessing |

## Questions past the polite answer

The first answer to "why are you leaving" is a courtesy. The useful answer is two
questions later, and it needs to be safe to give.

- What was the moment you first thought about leaving? (Finds the trigger, not the
  headline.)
- What would have had to change for you to stay? (Tests whether it was fixable here.)
- What did the new role offer that this one did not? (Converts "better opportunity" into a
  specific gap.)
- What should we have done differently six months ago? (Asks about the preventable part.)
- What would you tell a friend considering this team? (The candid summary.)
- What are you taking from here that you valued? (The thing to protect for others.)

Do not ask what the person's new salary is, and do not make the interview a negotiation.
It is a review, not a retention attempt; the retention attempt was months ago.

## Stated versus probable reasons

Record both columns. The gap between them is the finding.

| Stated | Probable, from the follow-up |
| --- | --- |
| "Better opportunity" | No visible next level, so the opportunity had to be elsewhere |
| "Personal reasons" | A flexibility need the role could not meet |
| "More money" | Pay fell behind the band eighteen months ago and was never corrected |
| "Time for a change" | A manager change left the person without a sponsor |

A pattern in the probable column is actionable; a pattern in the stated column usually is
not, because it is a courtesy. When a leaver will not go past the stated reason, record
"unknown" and move on rather than inventing a cause.

## The cuts that reveal a cluster

A company-wide attrition rate hides every cluster. Cut the regretted voluntary losses by
each of these and look for one that stands out.

- **Team** — a team losing at twice the baseline is a finding about that team.
- **Manager** — losses clustered under one manager, especially across different teams over
  time.
- **Level** — a level losing people is usually a growth or pay structure problem.
- **Tenure** — under a year is hiring and onboarding; two to four years is growth and pay;
  five-plus is usually a change in the work or the company.
- **Recency** — a spike in the last two quarters against a flat prior year is a change,
  and the change is usually identifiable.
- **The people you wanted to keep** — regretted losses on their own, since the full set is
  diluted by exits you did not mind.

## A worked year

A 40-person engineering group, baseline attrition 12%.

| Cut | Count | Rate | Reading |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| All voluntary | 7 | 17.5% | Above baseline, but the mix matters |
| Regretted voluntary | 4 | 10% | The retention signal |
| Not regretted | 3 | 7.5% | Managed or welcome exits; not retention |
| Senior and staff | 3 of 4 regretted | — | Structural, not individual |
| Under one manager | 3 of 4 regretted | — | A cluster: examine the manager, not the leavers |
| Tenure 2–4 years | 4 of 4 regretted | — | Growth and pay, not onboarding |

The reading here is not "attrition is up". It is that four regretted losses sit in one
manager's team, at senior level, at two-to-four years' tenure — which points at a growth
path and a band in that area rather than at the individuals. That is the finding a rate
alone would have hidden, and it is what the actions should address.
