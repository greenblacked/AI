# Mentoring Cycle

A mentoring relationship is working when, after three months, the mentee can name what changed because of it, and both people chose to continue, change focus or stop rather than drift.

The usual failure is a pleasant chat that repeats monthly. Nobody set a goal, so nothing can be missed; the mentor fills the time with stories and advice nobody asked for; the mentee arrives with no agenda and leaves with no action. Everything below gives the conversation a contract, an owner and an output.

## Scope

Use for: a mentor or mentee setting up, running or repairing a relationship that lasts months and sits outside the mentee's reporting line.

Do not use for: a manager's evidence-based review and development plan (`growth-review`); one specific hard conversation (`difficult-conversation`); the plan for a new joiner, including who the buddy is (`onboarding-plan`); a single hands-on teaching session on one task (`teaching-pairing`).

## Workflow

### 1. Hold a contract session first

The first meeting produces no advice. It produces a short written contract, owned by both:

- At most two goals. Phrase each as something the mentee will be able to do or decide, not a topic. Two is a ceiling because a third goal dilutes every session into a survey.
- Cadence: every two weeks, 45 minutes, booked as a series for three months so the slot survives a busy week.
- Confidentiality: what is said stays between the two, with stated exceptions that follow the employer's policy and the mentor's own obligations (a safety or legal matter, and any reporting duty that comes with seniority). Say the exceptions aloud. This is procedure, not HR or legal advice; check the policy if unsure.
- How to end: either side can stop at any time, or at a checkpoint, by saying so, with no reason owed. Agree the sentence in advance ("I think this has run its course for me") so using it costs nothing.
- The mentor's role: a sounding board, not an evaluator. Say that the mentor does not report on the mentee to the employer, within the exceptions above.

Read `references/contract-and-session-templates.md` for the contract template.

### 2. The mentee owns the agenda

The mentee sends the agenda 24 hours before each session: one to three items, each phrased as a question or a decision, plus a line on what happened since last time. A mentor who must invent the agenda has become the manager of the relationship, and a mentee who cannot produce one is telling you the goals are wrong or the slot is not wanted. A late or empty agenda is a signal to raise at the next checkpoint, not to cover with a monologue.

### 3. Open by reviewing actions

Start every session with the actions from last time: done, not done, and what was learned either way. Take two minutes, not ten. This is the only mechanism that turns conversation into change; skip it twice and the actions stop being written.

### 4. Mentor: ask before telling

Work an item up a question ladder, one rung at a time, and wait through the silence after each:

1. What have you tried so far?
2. What do you expect to happen, and why?
3. What would you do if I were not here?
4. What would change your mind?

Only after the mentee has answered rung 4 do you offer your view, labelled as one experience rather than the answer ("what I did, in a different company, was..."). Advice comes at the end of an item because given early it ends the thinking. The test of a good session is that the mentee spoke for most of it.

### 5. Close with at most two written actions

In the last five minutes the mentee states and writes the actions, each with a date before the next session. Two is a ceiling: more than two is a wish list and the first item to slip is the important one. The mentee sends the list to the mentor the same day. Actions belong to the mentee; a mentor action (an introduction, a document to share) is allowed but counts toward the two.

### 6. Hold a three-month checkpoint

At the end of the series, spend a session on three questions: what has changed against the goals, what has the cadence been in practice, and should this continue, change focus or end. Choose one of the three and write it down. Ending after a checkpoint is a success, not a failure. Changing focus restarts at step 1 with new goals and a new three-month series.

### 7. Keep the boundary

The mentor does not evaluate the mentee for the employer, does not pass on what was said, and does not become a second manager. If the mentee asks for a promotion opinion, the mentor shares observations with the mentee only and sends the evaluation question to the manager. If the mentee wants a hard conversation with their own manager, help them prepare it but do not hold it for them. Read `references/mentee-and-mentor-checks.md` for repair steps when it is going wrong.

## Hand back

The written contract (goals, cadence, confidentiality, exit); for a running relationship the last two sessions' agendas and actions; the checkpoint decision with its reason; and for a relationship in trouble, the single change to try before the next session.

## Anti-patterns

- **Monthly chat with no goal.** Pleasant, forgettable and impossible to end cleanly.
- **The mentor tells first.** The mentee leaves with an answer to a question they never formed.
- **No action review.** Without it, the actions are decoration.
- **Five actions.** A list the mentee will not finish, with the important one at the bottom.
- **Mentor as informant.** One report to the employer ends the candour for good.
- **Drifting on.** A relationship with no checkpoint outlives its usefulness, and then ends by silence.
