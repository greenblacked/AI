# Reading Backlog

A backlog is under control when the queue is short enough to name from memory, every item in it serves a stated task this quarter, and the number you finish each month is higher than it was.

The usual failure is treating the pile as a debt. Saving feels like progress, so the list grows faster than anyone reads; each new item adds guilt, and the guilt makes opening the list unpleasant, so reading falls further behind. Counting is the first move because a vague mountain feels unbounded, and a number can be cut. Everything below shrinks the queue by decision, not by willpower.

## Scope

Use for: a pile of saved links, unread books, a folder of PDFs or papers, a read-later app with hundreds of items; choosing what to finish and what to drop.

Do not use for: turning something you have just read into a note worth keeping (`learning-notes`); the general weekly planning reset (`weekly-review`); building a nightly reading habit, which is a behaviour question (`habit-change`).

## Workflow

### 1. Count and date it

List every source: the read-later app, browser bookmarks, the book shelf, the downloads folder, starred emails, open tabs. For each item note the format (article, paper, book, video) and the date saved. Where an export has no date, mark the age unknown and treat the item as older than 90 days; an export's file date is only the day it was exported. The output is one table and two numbers: total items, and items older than 90 days. Do not start reading while counting; the point is to see the size. Read `references/triage-sheet.md` for the sheet and the export hints.

### 2. Declare bankruptcy on the old

Move everything saved more than 90 days ago into an archive: a folder, a tag or a separate list, searchable and out of the queue. An item that has sat for a quarter without being opened has told you its real priority. The archive is not a deletion; if a task later needs a topic, search the archive then. Do this in one sitting, in bulk, without reviewing each item, because reviewing is how the pile survives. No apology, no sorting.

### 3. Tie each survivor to a task

For each remaining item write one line: the task or decision this quarter it serves ("choose a queue for the billing rewrite", "prepare the March talk"). If you cannot name one, archive it. Topics of general interest are allowed only in a small, named allowance, such as one book at a time; everything else needs a task. A reading list organised by interest grows forever; one organised by tasks ends when the tasks do.

### 4. Limit work in progress to three

At most three items open at once, counting a book, a paper or a long article series each as one. Open means started and not finished or dropped. A fourth cannot start until one is finished or dropped. Open items compete for the same attention, and three is enough to have a choice of mood without scattering.

### 5. One in, one out

The queue holds at most ten items, a figure you can scan at a glance; keep it separate from the three-item open limit in step 4, which counts only what is started. A new addition is allowed while the queue holds fewer than ten; when it is full, it replaces one, which leaves by being finished, dropped or archived. If the queue is still over ten after steps 2 and 3, archive from the bottom until it is ten. The cap is on the queue, not on curiosity: you may still save a link to the archive in one tap. This turns "I will read this later" into a decision at the moment of saving, when the cost of deciding is lowest.

### 6. Set the abandon rule

Quit a book that has not paid off by 10% of its length, and an article, report or paper that has not paid off by 30 pages. "Paid off" means it has told you something relevant to its named task. Dropping is a result, recorded with a line on why, and it frees a work-in-progress slot. Read the introduction and conclusion of a paper before committing to the body; the rule is for when it still disappoints.

### 7. Put reading in the calendar

Book slots sized to the format, not "some reading time": 25 minutes for an article, 45 to 60 for a paper, a recurring 30 minutes for a book chapter. Slots are booked against the three open items. Reading outside a slot is a bonus; reading that exists only as a hope does not happen. Read `references/slots-and-weekly-pass.md` for sizing and the weekly pass.

### 8. Run a ten-minute weekly pass

Once a week, ten minutes, take every item that arrived and every open item and put each in one of three places: read (move into a slot this week), schedule (a named slot or a quarter-task), or archive. The pass ends with the queue at ten items or fewer and no more than three open. If it takes more than ten minutes the queue is too long; archive harder.

### 9. Measure items finished per month

Track one number: items finished (or deliberately dropped) per month, by format. Do not track items saved; it rewards the behaviour that made the pile. Look at the number at the end of the month: if it is flat, shorten the queue or the slots, not the standards. Compare against the first count in step 1.

## Hand back

The count table (total, over 90 days, by format); the archive location; the current queue with one task per item; the three open items; the slot schedule for the next two weeks; the abandon rule in your own words; and the baseline for items finished per month.

## Anti-patterns

- **Reading to clear the pile.** The oldest items are the least relevant; archive them.
- **Interest-based lists.** They never end. Tie items to tasks.
- **Ten books open.** Each one blocks the others, and none gets finished.
- **Deleting out of guilt, then re-saving.** Archive instead; it costs nothing and removes the fear of loss.
- **Finishing out of obligation.** A dropped book is a saved evening.
- **Counting saves.** The metric that created the problem cannot measure the fix.
