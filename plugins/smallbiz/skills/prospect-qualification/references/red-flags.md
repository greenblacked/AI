# Red flags

Each flag predicts one of two losses: not being paid, or work expanding beyond what was agreed. A flag is a reason to ask a question, not a verdict.

| Flag | What it predicts | Question that tests it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| No budget, or refusal of a range | Price shopping; the client will anchor on the lowest bid | "To suggest the right approach, what range have you set aside, even roughly?" |
| Unnamed or unreachable decision-maker | Late approvals, rework after a senior person weighs in, delayed payment | "Who will sign off, and can they join a short call?" |
| "Quick favour" or "an hour's work" | Scope that is minimised now and grows later | "What does done look like? I will list the deliverables." |
| Refuses a deposit or staged payments | Payment risk; the client expects to hold all the leverage | "My start terms are a deposit and staged invoices. Is that workable for your finance team?" |
| Spec work or free sample | Risk moved to you, ideas used without payment | See the substitutes in SKILL.md step 4 |
| History of dropped contractors | A pattern in the client, not in the contractors | "What went wrong with the previous supplier, and what would you change this time?" |
| Deadline with no driver | Urgency used as pressure; deadlines that move when it suits them | "What happens if it lands two weeks later?" |

## Reading the answers

- A good answer is specific and slightly inconvenient: a named person, a figure, a date.
- A bad answer is smooth and short, or a change of subject.
- Hostility to a reasonable question (a deposit, a signed scope) is itself data and counts as a flag if it was not already.

## Counting

Count distinct flags, not repeats of one. Two flags trigger the gate in SKILL.md step 3. Write the count in the lead log with the date, so a later argument that "you never mentioned it" has a record.
